We're Too Different. Or Are We?

The differences that once drew two people together can start to feel like a threat. They may also be where they meet.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

At some point in many relationships one partner says it aloud: “We’re just too different.” Sometimes it comes in the middle of a fight. Sometimes it arrives quietly, after years of the same argument about money, in-laws, sex, holidays, how to raise the children. It sounds like a diagnosis. It often feels like the beginning of an ending.

The sentence is common in couples work, and it deserves to be taken seriously. It also deserves some curiosity, because in my experience difference is rarely the whole story.

Where the attraction began

Very often what first drew one partner to the other was precisely the way they were unlike them. Spontaneous where the other was careful. Calm where the other was intense. From a different country, a different kind of family, a different way of laughing. That otherness enlarges a world, and for a while it is the most interesting thing about the other person.

As relationships deepen, something shifts. The difference that once excited us can begin to feel like a threat. In my book on couples therapy I describe how psychological forces pull partners towards sameness as closeness grows, partly because, throughout human history, the unfamiliar has signalled danger (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). What was a spark becomes friction.

Couples respond in a few recognisable ways. One partner keeps giving in, and slowly disappears. Both withdraw into parallel lives arranged around the sore spots. Or each sets about turning the other into someone more reasonable, which usually means someone more like themselves. That last move is where difference reliably turns into conflict.

Two who become none

Many of us carry an old picture of love in which two people become one. It is a beautiful image, and in many cultures it has held families and communities together for a long time. The Gestalt therapist Bob Resnick (2020) points to its hidden cost: when two truly become one, there is none.

Consider a hand placed in water at exactly body temperature. After a moment there is no telling where skin ends and water begins. Without a difference there is no edge, and without an edge there is nothing to feel. Couples who aim at perfect agreement can find they are barely able to sense each other at all. Resnick describes the alternative as “a couple of individuals”: two people who remain distinct and choose, again and again, to meet.

Contact happens at the edge where two people are different.

This is what I mean by the dance of difference. Differences are the material a relationship is made from. They are where two people actually touch.

Autonomy and togetherness

Closeness comes and goes, and in a working relationship that movement is the point. Partners come close, step back to gather themselves, then come close again. Resnick’s model describes a rhythm of withdrawal, contact, intimacy and merging, with isolation as the place couples arrive at when the movement stops. Withdrawal belongs to the rhythm. It restores the self, much as sleep restores the body.

Trouble tends to start when partners are out of step. One wants closeness just as the other needs space. The one reaching out feels abandoned; the one pulling back feels crowded, and each can read the other’s need as rejection. Underneath there is usually a history: a child who learned that quiet meant danger, or one who learned that being close meant being swallowed. Those histories make sense, and they can be worked with once both partners see them.

Shame lives at this edge too. To show a partner who we really are, including the parts that differ from them, is exposing, and many of us find it easier to agree, or to go silent, than to be seen and found wanting. Much of couples work is concerned with making that exposure bearable, so that each person can say what is true for them and stay in the conversation.

Across cultures

Singapore has many couples who come from different countries, languages, religions and family systems. For them, “we’re too different” can carry extra weight. One partner grew up where family decisions are made together and parents are consulted about everything. The other grew up where adult children are expected to decide alone. Neither is wrong. Each carries a whole world of assumptions that feel like common sense until they meet the other’s.

In these couples each partner is frequently waiting, quietly, for the other to adapt. Once the waiting is named and what each one is protecting becomes clear, the argument often softens. Loyalty to a mother can be honoured without being obeyed in every particular. A need for privacy can be respected without being read as coldness. The difference remains. What changes is how it is carried. Many couples find that the difference they feared becomes something they can be curious about, and sometimes proud of.

What couples therapy addresses

Couples therapy is concerned less with who is right than with how two people meet, or miss each other. It attends to what happens between partners as they speak, since the pattern tends to appear in the sessions before long and can then be examined while it is running. The work proceeds in phases and at a pace the couple sets: first what recurs between them, then what it costs, then whatever each has been keeping out of sight. Sessions are 80 minutes and private enough that the things left unsaid at home can be said in them. Often something shifts when each partner can be more fully themselves in front of the other, and finds that the relationship survives it. You can read more on the couples counselling page.

Which leaves a question worth considering before any conclusion about compatibility. When a couple says they are too different, are they describing the distance between them, or the effort of pretending it was not there?

Questions

Common questions

Can couples therapy help if two people want very different things?

Often, yes. Couples therapy does not aim to produce agreement on everything. It helps each partner understand what they are protecting and what they need, and find ways to stay connected while remaining distinct. Sometimes the gap turns out to be smaller than it seemed. Sometimes a couple reaches a clearer decision about their future.

We come from different cultures. Will the therapist take sides?

No. I work in Singapore with many cross-cultural couples, having trained and practised in Vienna before moving here. The assumptions each partner brings are treated as material to be understood, including my own. The aim is that both are heard, so that neither culture becomes the standard the other is measured against.

What if one partner does not want to come?

That is common. Individual sessions are a legitimate way to examine one side of a relationship, and doing so often changes the pattern between two people. A reluctant partner sometimes joins later, once it is clear the work is not an inquiry into blame. Couples sessions are 80 minutes, which gives both partners time to speak.
