The Conversation You Haven't Had

Most stuck decisions have a person in them. Voicing both sides of that conversation often does what further deliberation cannot.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

A decision has been sitting in someone's life for two years. Whether to leave the job. Whether to leave the marriage. Whether to say the thing to a father before he gets much older. Whether to move the children, or change schools, or stop trying for another.

The list of pros and cons has been made more than once. Friends have heard it until they are tired of it. Both sides can be argued so well that neither side wins. And then, often in passing, on the way to something else, the real trouble gets named. There is a conversation that has never been had.

Most stuck decisions have a person in them

Decisions of this kind are seldom arithmetic. The job decision has a manager in it, or a father who wanted a doctor. The marriage decision has a wife in it who has never been told one particular sentence. The decision about a parent has fifty years of household in it. While the other person's position is being carried silently, both halves of a conversation are running in one head, and one of the halves is a guess.

The guess is rarely neutral. It tends to be the harshest available version: the manager who will be contemptuous, the mother who will be devastated, the partner who will simply agree and prove that nothing mattered. Deliberation then proceeds against a character the deliberator has written.

The empty chair

Gestalt therapy has a well-established method for this, and it is old and very simple. A chair is placed opposite the person, the absent party is imagined in it, and the person speaks to them rather than about them. Speaking about someone keeps them at a distance at which they cannot answer. Later the person takes the other chair and replies as that person. Two chairs, two positions, one person giving voice to both. There is no script, and nothing mystical is claimed for it.

What the arrangement does is take an argument that has been conducted privately and put it into the room, where it can be heard rather than only thought. Awareness of what is happening as it happens is the working material of this kind of therapy (Perls, Hefferline & Goodman, 1951), and a private argument is difficult to be aware of from inside.

In my book on couples therapy I describe the empty chair as the most recognised and the most misunderstood element in Gestalt practice. Placed mechanically, without a feel for what is actually emerging, it becomes dead technique (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). An experiment earns its place by arising from what is already happening in the session, and it earns it again by being refusable. When someone declines it, or tries it and finds it hollow, that is information about what interrupts contact. I have learnt as much from experiments that went nowhere as from those that went well.

When it does not fit

Some people find the method odd, and it is better said here than discovered in a session. Speaking to a chair can feel theatrical. Clients in Singapore sometimes say it reminds them of drama class, and there is often a concern about appearing foolish in front of somebody whose good opinion matters to them. That concern is worth saying aloud, and it is frequently the most interesting thing in the hour.

The method is offered and it can be declined, at the start or halfway through. Other arrangements do the same work: the letter that will not be sent, speaking while looking out of a window, telling the therapist directly what would be said. The chair is one arrangement for a conversation. It is the conversation that does the work.

There is also a limit to observe. Work of this kind sometimes reaches material heavier than a career question, particularly where the person in the other chair caused real harm. That calls for a slower approach, and sometimes for leaving the method aside until there is enough ground under the work to carry it. Psychotherapy proceeds in phases, and an experiment belongs to the phase that can accommodate it.

What tends to follow when both sides are voiced

Several things tend to happen once both positions have been spoken aloud. The other person becomes specific. An objection that had been a general atmosphere of disapproval turns out to have actual words in it, and words can be answered.

A person's own position gets its full size. Many people are surprised by the force that arrives when a long-withheld sentence is finally said, and that force had been going into keeping it in.

Sometimes the tone shifts. From the other seat, people hear something they had never attributed to that voice: fear, or tiredness, or a wish that things had gone differently. A voice that had only ever sounded punishing turns out to have something else in it. The change that follows is usually quiet. It can look like being able to say "I can't" to expectations that have been running a calendar for twenty years.

The method does not aim at producing a decision, and I would not promise any particular feeling from it. It allows a person to occupy their actual position fully, and decisions tend to follow from that more readily than from further deliberation (Beisser, 1970). Some people leave a session still undecided and noticeably lighter, and make the call three weeks later without drama.

Gestalt coaching uses the same method for questions that are not symptoms: a career that has gone quiet, a business partnership, a marriage at a fork, the way a parent is handling a teenager who reminds them of themselves. There is an outline of the approach on my Gestalt therapy page.

When a decision has been stuck for a long time, the more useful question tends to concern the person on the other side of it, and what has never been said to them. That is usually where the deliberation has been going, and it is where the work starts.

Questions

Common questions

What actually happens in empty chair work?

A chair is placed opposite the client, who speaks directly to someone who is not present. After a while they may take that chair and answer as that person. Both positions get said aloud instead of argued privately. It usually takes ten or fifteen minutes, and what came up is discussed afterwards.

What if I find it embarrassing, or would rather not do it?

That is fine and fairly common. Experiments are offered, never required, and can be stopped at any point. The embarrassment itself is often worth a conversation, since it says something about being watched. There are other routes to the same material, including writing, or simply telling the therapist what would be said.

Is this coaching or therapy?

The method is the same; the focus differs. Coaching work centres on a decision or a role, such as a career move or a partnership. Therapy takes in longer-standing patterns and distress. Many people start with a decision and find older material underneath it, and where the work is going can be discussed openly.
