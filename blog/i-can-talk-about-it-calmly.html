I Can Talk About It Calmly. So Why Isn't It Over?

Fluent narration is an achievement. What keeps the past working in the present is reached slowly, at a pace a person can take.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Some people arrive already able to tell the story well. They have told it to friends, to a partner, perhaps to a counsellor or two. The facts are in order and the voice stays level. They can describe the accident, the violent household, the years of being bullied, the sudden death, and barely pause.

Then comes the question they actually came with. I have talked about it many times and I understand what happened. Why does it still run my life?

It is a fair question, and it tends to come from people who have already worked hard on their own history.

A story can become a surface

When something overwhelming happens, the mind looks for a way to hold it, and telling is one of the most human ways available. A story told many times goes smooth. It becomes a path the attention walks along, and the path keeps a workable distance from what it describes. The words stay accurate while the rest of the person stands back.

Fluent narration is often hard won and there is no reason to take it from anyone. It is also, frequently, a layer. It made complete sense when it formed and it still does some work. What it does not reach is the part of the difficulty that was never in words to begin with.

What happens when something old is touched

This part is worth stating plainly. When something in the present resembles something old, a person responds before anything has been decided. Heart rate changes. Breathing shortens, or goes very quiet. Muscles prepare. This occurs in a fraction of a second, ahead of any thought about whether the situation is dangerous, and it occurs whether or not the trigger can be identified.

What is stored is less a picture than a readiness. Bessel van der Kolk (2014) put it in a phrase many readers will know: the body keeps the score. In my own work I find it more useful to treat trauma as present tense rather than historical. It shows up as heaviness arriving without explanation, breath flattening, attention losing focus at the exact moment closeness is being asked for (Chew-Helbig, 2026a).

Up, or flat

Two things can happen from there, and people are often puzzled by the second. Arousal can rise: heat, speed, an audible heartbeat, the urge to argue or move or leave. That is mobilisation, and it makes obvious sense in a body preparing to act.

Or it can flatten. Heaviness. Sleepiness in the middle of an important conversation. Sound receding slightly. A sense of watching from a little way back. This is what happens when a person has learned that action is not available, because they were small, or because the other person was a parent, or because leaving would have cost more than staying. Shutting down was the intelligent option then, and it stays on the menu now.

One reason arguments go strange is that both responses can be present at once. One partner speeds up while the other goes flat, and each reads the other's state as indifference or as attack. In my book on couples work I describe patterns of this kind: drowsiness that a partner experiences as abandonment, vigilance that a partner experiences as suffocation (Chew-Helbig, 2026a).

Going away without leaving the room

Dissociation has a dramatic reputation and is usually undramatic from the inside. Most people do not lose time in large blocks. They notice that another person's voice seems further away than it was, or that they have been looking at the same spot on the floor, or that their hands seem to belong to someone standing slightly to one side. Several minutes go by with nothing in them. Afterwards there is often a heaviness, a gap, and a vague sense of having been unreachable.

People rarely mention it, partly because it sounds alarming and partly because it is hard to put into words. It is an ordinary protection, and it is information. Where it appears in a session it indicates that something of consequence has been reached, and that the pace has gone beyond what can currently be taken.

Pace is the method

So the work is paced, and the pacing is the method rather than a preliminary to it. Overwhelm teaches nothing new; it repeats the old lesson. What changes things is the other arrangement: going near the edge of what can be felt, staying a short while, and coming back with someone else still present.

In practice this is small. A detail is mentioned and something shifts—a change in breathing, a tightening somewhere, a flicker of feeling that arrives and goes. It gets noticed, stayed with for a few seconds, and then the session returns to the present and to ordinary surroundings. Later it may be approached again. Daniel Stern (2004) wrote about present moments, short stretches of a few seconds in which something between two people becomes real. Change in therapy tends to gather in moments of that kind rather than in the narrative.

A story is how someone survived. The body is where it still happens.

Trust is what makes any of this possible, and it accumulates slowly. I facilitated a workgroup of trauma researchers, psychotherapists themselves, reflecting on interviews they had conducted with survivors. What had stayed with them was frequently bodily: a heaviness, an urge to leave, a feeling that arrived before there were words for it (Tóth & Chew-Helbig, 2025). A calm account can carry that kind of message underneath it, and a therapist's business is to stay steady enough that it can come up at a rate the person can manage.

People sometimes expect a release, and that does happen. More often the change is gradual and slightly unglamorous. The story loses its polish. Something can be felt while it is being spoken about, and the speaker stays where they are. A reaction gets noticed a moment earlier than it used to be. A partner's silence is, more often, just silence. My page on Gestalt therapy describes the approach more generally.

Short-term counselling stabilises people and supplies ways to cope, which is real work. This is built for what remains once the story is already known and told well.

Questions

Common questions

Will I have to relive my trauma in detail?

No. There is no need to go through the story again in detail, and nobody is pushed into memories they are not ready for. The work attends to small moments in the present, such as a change in breathing or in the voice, and keeps returning to what steadies. Pace comes first.

What if someone goes numb or blank during a session?

That is common and useful to notice. Going distant or foggy is often how a person was protected, and it is treated with respect. When it happens the pace slows, attention returns to the room, and what came just before can be looked at. Over time many people find they can stay present a little longer.

I have already done counselling. Why would this be different?

Earlier counselling may have helped make sense of what happened and supplied ways to cope, which is real work. This approach attends to what is happening in the present, in the body and in the relationship, while a person speaks. It suits those for whom the understanding is already there and the pattern continues anyway.
