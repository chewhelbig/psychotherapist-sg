When One of You Has Already Half-Left

One of you is leaning out, one leaning in. What a few honest sessions can clarify while the decision is still open.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Decisions about a marriage are rarely made on the day they are announced. By the time somebody says they no longer know whether they want this, they have often been saying it privately for a year or more, in the car, at three in the morning, in the gap between one obligation and the next. The announcement is the end of a long internal process and the beginning of a shared one. That gap is why so many couples come to therapy already facing different ways.

Leaning out, leaning in

William Doherty and colleagues call these mixed-agenda couples: one partner leaning out of the relationship, the other leaning in (Doherty et al., 2016). It is a common position, and it makes ordinary couples counselling hard to begin, because the two of them want different things from it. The partner leaning in wants to work on the marriage. The partner leaning out may feel that agreeing to therapy is already a promise they are unable to make.

The one who is half out usually carries guilt, and a tiredness built up over years. Many have asked for change repeatedly and then stopped asking. The stopping is generally the significant moment, and it tends to pass unnoticed at the time. There can be shame as well: at being the one who breaks the family, at what parents and friends will say. In Singapore, where family and community frequently have a strong voice in a marriage, those absent witnesses can feel very present in the conversation.

The one leaning in is often in shock, and may feel ambushed, frightened, humiliated. The urge is to act at once—to promise, to plead, to change overnight—and that urgency is understandable. It also tends to push the other partner further out, because it asks for a decision before one is ready.

Sometimes neither partner is clearly in or out. Both are exhausted, and the marriage has become a set of logistics: school runs, bills, the helper’s day off. Shared ambivalence usually goes unspoken, because saying it aloud feels like starting an ending. Naming it is often a relief, and it is frequently where the real conversation begins.

Therapy that does not campaign

Couples in this position sometimes assume a therapist’s job is to save the marriage. It is not. In my book on couples work I put it plainly: the process “doesn’t advocate for any predetermined outcome, whether reconciliation or divorce” (Chew-Helbig, 2026a).

That stance is a working condition rather than a shrug. Clarity is difficult to manufacture. The partner leaning out usually has to voice the ambivalence in full, including whatever part of them still hopes, before they can tell what they want. The partner leaning in usually has to feel the grief and anger of the moment instead of pouring everything into a rescue. Both become harder if either of them suspects the therapist is quietly rooting for an outcome. A room with an agenda tends to produce performances, and performances are what many couples in this position have been living on for years.

The centred position

Gestalt therapy has a name for the position the therapist works to keep: creative indifference. It comes from the philosopher Salomo Friedlaender, and the English word misleads. It describes a centred place between two opposing pulls, a zero point from which both can be felt without collapsing into either (Chew-Helbig, 2022b). It is active and attentive, closer to poise than to detachment. Friedlaender’s zero point is a place of possibility. From it, something neither partner planned sometimes appears: a sentence finally said, a grief finally felt, a decision that arrives with less violence than either of them feared.

Keeping that position is work, and it is the therapist’s work rather than the couple’s. Each partner will at some point try to recruit the therapist to their side. Most people do, and it is better named aloud than left to operate silently. Holding the centre does not mean staying vague. There are moments when one partner’s needs have to wait, and moments when something uncomfortable has to be said plainly. What a centred position rules out is a hidden agenda.

What a few sessions can clarify

When the future is undecided, a short and clearly bounded piece of work often does more than open-ended couples therapy. Three or four sessions can establish what each partner actually wants, as distinct from what they have been arguing for. They can separate the question of whether someone wants this marriage from the question of whether they can bear the humiliation of being left, which in practice are often tangled together. They can make the pattern visible: who reaches, who retreats, what each one does at the moment it gets close. And they can test something specific, which is whether a real conversation is still possible between the two of them. That is a different question from whether they still love each other, and often a more useful one.

It is agreed at the outset that no decision is due at the end. Some couples arrive at one anyway. Having finally spoken, some find they want to try again, and the work becomes a deeper kind of couples therapy. Others see that the marriage is over, and that too can be done with care. Where there are children, a marriage may end while the relationship between two parents continues for decades, and how a couple parts shapes what follows. Partners sometimes speak more truthfully as a marriage ends than they managed for years inside it, and there is a strange tenderness in that.

If joint sessions are not bearable yet, individual work is available to either partner, and the one leaning out is frequently the person with nowhere to think. The sessions are private, unhurried and under no obligation to reach a conclusion. You can read more on the couples counselling page, or about what a first consultation involves.

Undecided is a position, not a failure to hold one. Most people in it are waiting for certainty to arrive and settle the matter for them. In my experience the decision tends to come the other way round: someone says the thing they have been carrying, feels what it costs to say it, and discovers afterwards that they already knew. The clarity follows the speaking, which is an argument for speaking sooner than feels comfortable.

Questions

Common questions

Can couples therapy help when one partner wants to leave?

It can, provided it is not a disguised attempt to change anyone’s mind. Therapy gives both partners a place to understand what has happened and where each of them stands. From there, some couples choose to work on the relationship and others decide to part. Either way the decision tends to be clearer, and kinder to both.

Will the therapist try to save the marriage?

No. The aim is a centred position between two partners, without an agenda for staying or leaving. What is seen is sometimes named directly, and the decision still belongs to the couple. Holding that centre is what allows each partner to speak about ambivalence, hope and hurt without being pushed towards an answer.

How many sessions does it take to get clear?

Often fewer than people expect. Three or four sessions can be enough to establish where each partner stands and whether a real conversation is still possible between them. It is agreed at the start that no decision is due at the end. Some couples then continue; others stop there and decide in their own time.
