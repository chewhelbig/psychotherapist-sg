I Know Why I Do It. So Why Do I Keep Doing It?

Understanding a pattern and changing it happen in different places. Change tends to follow contact with what is happening now.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Someone sits down and says it plainly. They have thought about this for years. They know their father's temper made them watchful. They know why they go quiet when a partner raises their voice, why they over-prepare for every meeting, why they reach for the phone when the flat is too quiet. They can trace the pattern to its roots and name every trigger. And it continues.

This is one of the most common places to arrive, and one of the most frustrating. It often follows a round of counselling that did useful work. Understanding is valuable. It gives a story to what once felt like chaos. Yet understanding alone seldom changes a reflex, and many thoughtful people take that as a personal failure. They conclude they have not understood enough, or have not tried hard enough.

Why knowing seldom changes the pattern

Patterns of this kind are usually learned in the body long before they are thought about. A child who grew quiet to avoid a parent's anger did not decide to. The breath shortened, the shoulders rose, the voice went somewhere far away. In Gestalt therapy we call this a creative adjustment: the best available response to a situation that was once overwhelming (Perls, Hefferline & Goodman, 1951). It worked. That is why it stayed.

Insight and the pattern occupy different territory. Many people can describe a freeze fluently while going still in the telling. The description is made at a distance from the experience, rather as one might study a map of a city never walked through. Change appears to ask for something closer than that.

The trouble with trying harder

The usual response to a disliked habit is effort. Resolutions are made. Rules are set. Another book is read. Often the harder the push, the more firmly the old habit holds, because one part of a person is now straining against another, and the struggle itself becomes familiar ground.

Arnold Beisser, a psychiatrist who trained with Fritz Perls, wrote a short essay that has shaped my work. Change, he argued, comes less from trying, persuasion or insight than from a person stopping, at least for the moment, reaching for what they would like to become, and allowing themselves to be what they are (Beisser, 1970). This became known as the paradoxical theory of change. The name sounds grand; the idea is modest. A place we have never fully arrived at is difficult to leave.

In my book on couples therapy I describe how the harder partners try to force themselves or each other to be different, the more entrenched their struggles become (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). Something similar holds inside one person. Striving keeps us at war with ourselves. Awareness lets the war be seen.

There is often a quiet shame in the cycle. Each repetition becomes further evidence that something is wrong, and the shame adds its own weight to the next attempt. Some of us are guilty of treating ourselves as projects that keep failing, and the treatment is rarely effective. Seen from the paradoxical view, the repetition is the pattern still doing its old job, because nothing has yet been allowed to take its place.

What experiential therapy attends to

Psychotherapy of this kind is less interested in why a person does something than in how they do it, while they are doing it. Many of us narrate our difficulties expertly and at speed, which keeps them at a workable remove. Slowing that down makes a pattern available while it is still live: the drop in the voice, the change in breathing, the moment at which a sentence is swallowed. What becomes available then is the reflex itself rather than a report of it.

That is the plain description of the method, and it is as much as needs saying about mechanics here. When a person is in fuller contact with a freeze, including the fear under it and the child who once needed it, the freeze has less work to do. It no longer has to keep everything down on its own. People often describe a loosening they had not planned for, and behaviour tends to change afterwards without being forced.

The work is conducted in the present, because the present is where a pattern can be met while it is still alive. History matters, and it is discussed at length. It matters most in the form it takes today.

Accepting what is

Acceptance can sound like resignation. It is closer to curiosity. It means being willing to feel what a pattern protects against, rather than managing around it. That takes support, and it takes time, which is one reason this kind of therapy usually runs longer than a handful of sessions. There is an outline of the approach on my page on Gestalt therapy. Short-term counselling is well suited to building understanding and coping strategies, and many people get what they need from it. Experiential work is built for the gap that remains when a person understands everything and still finds themselves doing the thing.

Psychotherapy proceeds in phases and at a pace the person sets, from the surface downwards. Early sessions cover the ground a good counsellor would cover. What comes later is slower, and it is where the difference between explaining a pattern and altering it tends to appear.

Which leaves a real choice. Effort can be applied to a pattern that is already well understood, and for some people that does eventually shift something. The alternative is to spend time finding out what the pattern is built to overcome, which is slower and asks a person to feel what they have been managing around. Many of those who arrive at a psychotherapist's door have already given the first option several years.

Questions

Common questions

I have already had counselling and I understand my issues. Why would more therapy help?

Understanding is a real achievement, and it often comes from good counselling. Experiential therapy works at a different level. Rather than adding explanation, it attends to how a pattern happens while it is happening, in the body and in the conversation. Many people find that contact with the living pattern is what allows it to change.

What is the paradoxical theory of change?

It is an idea from the psychiatrist Arnold Beisser, central to Gestalt therapy. Change tends to happen when we stop forcing ourselves towards who we think we should be and become fully aware of who we are now. Struggling against a pattern often keeps it in place. Meeting it with curiosity gives it room to move.

Does Gestalt therapy ignore my childhood?

No. History matters a great deal. It is approached through the present, through the form old experience takes today in the body, in feeling, and in how a person relates to others. The past becomes something that can be worked with directly, rather than only a story told about oneself.
