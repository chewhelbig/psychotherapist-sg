Why Do I Turn Against Myself When Things Go Wrong?

The harsh voice can be described rather than argued with, and the describing is where something loosens.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

A message arrives. Four words long, flat in tone. Before we have finished deciding what it means, something else has already started. You should have known. You always do this. The sentences come faster than thought, in a voice that sounds like our own, and within a minute the message is no longer the subject. The subject is our character.

Or a project fails, or a friend goes cold, and the same sequence runs. It is quick. It is rarely proportionate to the event. And it often continues long after everyone else involved has considered the matter closed.

A phenomenon rather than a verdict

The critic is difficult to argue with. Argument gives it an opponent, and it has the better material. Agreement abandons the person. What tends to be more useful is to stop treating it as the truth about someone and to treat it as a phenomenon: something that occurs, with a shape and a timing, and that can therefore be described.

Described closely, it has features. There is usually something small in the half-second before the first sentence: a face that did not change, a pause in a conversation, a name missing from a list. The trigger is generally smaller than the response, and noticing that disproportion is already a kind of distance.

It has a location. Many people point to the chest, the throat, the stomach, the back of the neck. Some notice heat in the face and the eyes going down. Others notice a flattening instead, a sort of going away. The body tends to know before any sentence forms.

And it has words, which are often stranger said aloud than they feel from inside. You are disgusting. You ruin everything. It can be startling how extreme the language is, and how little it resembles anything we would say to another person. Whose voice it is can wait. That question usually answers itself once the tone has been heard from outside.

Then there is the question worth the most. What does it protect? If the voice went quiet for ten minutes, what would occupy the space instead? The answers tend to be specific. Anger at someone loved. Grief for which there is no room. A helplessness with nowhere to go.

From what I did to what I am

Helen Block Lewis drew a distinction that remains useful. In guilt, attention stays on the thing done. In shame, the whole self comes under review (Lewis, 1971). I made a mistake becomes I am a mistake. The shift is quick and mostly wordless, and it shows in the body before it shows in a sentence.

Gershen Kaufman described how shame, repeated often enough, becomes internalised, so that a person goes on shaming themselves long after anyone else has stopped (Kaufman, 1989). In many families here and elsewhere, criticism was also the ordinary language of care. A parent named what could be better because they wanted a child to do well, and praise was held back in case it bred complacency. The intention may have been loving. What can still be learned is that being seen means being found lacking, and that it is safer to get there first.

What the attack was for

Turning against oneself usually served a purpose once. A child who depends on adults who are unpredictable, cold or stretched too thin faces a choice made without words. Either the people I need are failing me, or I am the problem. For a child, the second is often easier to live with. If I am the problem, there is something to be done about it. If they are, the child is alone.

So the child takes the blame and keeps the bond. It is a clever solution, and adults carry it forward. When something goes wrong now, it can still feel less dangerous to turn on oneself than to feel anger towards someone who matters, or to face how little control there was. Self-sabotage sometimes works the same way. Failing early, on one's own terms, can feel more bearable than being rejected on someone else's.

Seen this way, the critic looks less like an enemy and more like an old guard still standing at a post that was abandoned years ago. That does not make it kind, and nobody is obliged to keep it. It does explain why shouting it down achieves so little. I understand shame as something that arises between people even where it appears to live entirely inside one person, which is why it tends to alter in company rather than in private review (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). There is more about that on my page on therapy for shame.

Psychotherapy addresses this at the level of the pattern rather than the incident: where the tone was learned, what it holds in place, and what has had no room. The work proceeds in phases, at a pace the person sets, and sessions are private and steady enough that the things the critic forbids can be said there first.

If it has gone further

For some people, turning against themselves includes thoughts of self-harm, or has at times become self-harm. Where that is part of a person's experience it deserves care, and it can be spoken about in therapy without judgement.

If you are thinking of harming yourself, or you are in crisis right now, please reach out now. In Singapore you can call the Samaritans of Singapore 24-hour hotline on 1767, or the National Mental Health Helpline on 1771. If you are in immediate danger, call 995. There is no need to wait until things get worse.

What changes

People often ask how long it takes for the voice to stop. In my experience it rarely stops altogether. What changes is the distance. The sentences still arrive, at roughly the same speed, and there is now a small gap in which they can be looked at rather than believed. Someone described it as hearing the weather report instead of standing out in the rain. In that gap, a failed project is a failed project. Painful, worth learning from, and no longer evidence about who a person is.

Questions

Common questions

Why is my inner critic so harsh?

Often because it learned its tone from early relationships and has gone on repeating it. It may also be protecting something by keeping the blame close to home, such as a bond with people who were depended upon. In my work, describing how the voice operates tends to soften it more than arguing with it does.

What is the difference between guilt and shame?

Guilt usually concerns something done and points towards repair. Shame concerns who a person feels they are, a sense of being wrong or unwanted as a whole. Guilt says I made a mistake. Shame says I am the mistake. Shame is often felt in the body well before it can be put into words.

What should I do if my self-criticism turns into thoughts of self-harm?

Please reach out now. In Singapore, call the Samaritans of Singapore 24-hour hotline on 1767 or the National Mental Health Helpline on 1771, and call 995 if you are in immediate danger. These thoughts can also be explored in therapy, with care and without judgement, once you are safe.
