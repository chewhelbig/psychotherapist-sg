What Happens When Nothing Seems to Be Happening in Therapy

Why deeper therapy moves slowly, and why the quiet stretches are part of the work rather than a delay before it.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

People considering longer-term therapy often ask, in one form or another, what they would be paying for during the quiet parts. It is a reasonable question. Accounts of depth work tend to emphasise insight and change, and say less about the stretches in which the conversation slows, the prepared material runs out, and a session appears to consist of two people sitting in a room.

Those stretches are not a pause before the work. In this way of working they are often where it happens, and it is worth explaining why.

Why the pace is slow on purpose

At conversational speed a person can stay ahead of themselves. Accounts are given fluently, difficulties are summarised, and the patterns that brought someone to therapy run so smoothly that there is nothing to see. Many of us are practised at this. Describing a difficulty well is a real skill, and it can keep the difficulty at a workable distance for years.

Slow the pace and the machinery becomes visible, usually first as discomfort. A pause with another person is not neutral for everyone. Many of us learned early that silence meant something had gone wrong: a parent's anger, a withdrawal, an absence about to become permanent. Decades later, a gap in a conversation can still produce a small and quite old alarm.

What we do with that alarm is the interesting part. Some of us reach for a topic, some apologise before there is anything to apologise for, some fill the space with a story that will do. That manoeuvre is usually the same one we perform elsewhere in life, with colleagues, with partners, with parents. It tends to be more informative than the subject it was going to rescue the hour with.

The fertile void

Gestalt therapy inherited a name for this territory from the philosopher Salomo Friedlaender, who wrote in 1918 about a still centre between opposites (Friedlaender, 1918). Take any pair of poles. Speaking and not speaking. Moving closer and pulling away. Wanting something and refusing to want it. Friedlaender was interested in the point between them, before either has been chosen, which he called the zero point and described as the nothingness that gives birth to worlds. I have written about what that idea offers a working therapist (Chew-Helbig, 2022b).

Gestalt therapists call the same place the fertile void. Void because it is empty, and there is no honest way around how uncomfortable that can be. Fertile because what has not yet been decided is still open. Remaining there asks a certain discipline of the clinician, since the urge to be useful is strong and easy to mistake for good practice. A therapist who fills every pause with a question or an interpretation will produce a tidier session and a shallower one.

What arrives late

Sentences that come out of a long pause tend to differ from the ones before it. They are quieter, less organised, and often followed by some version of I did not know I was going to say that. They could not have been reached at conversational speed, because conversational speed depends on knowing roughly what one is about to say.

Daniel Stern called the smallest units of lived experience present moments: a few seconds in which something shifts between two people (Stern, 2004). A look held a beat longer than usual. A sentence that lands, and both people know it has landed. Moments of this kind are easy to miss and difficult to schedule, and they need some room around them.

Not all of what matters is spoken. Analysing a filmed Gestalt session line by line, I found a second layer running beneath the content, an implicit action in how two people handled each other as they talked (Chew-Helbig, 2019). Someone who manages a therapist with careful politeness may be showing how they manage everyone they depend on. Someone who leaves long silences and then apologises for them may be showing what closeness has cost before. That layer is only legible when there is time for it to appear.

What this means for someone deciding

Depth work proceeds in phases, and the pace is set by the person doing it. Early sessions usually move at an ordinary conversational rhythm, because there is history to give and a working relationship to establish. Quieter stretches arrive later, and they tend to indicate that the surface material has been covered rather than that the therapy has stalled. Change in this kind of work follows from attending to what is actually present rather than from pressing towards what ought to be present instead (Beisser, 1970). That is less a theory than a practical statement about speed.

Nothing here needs to be taken on trust. It is fair to ask a therapist how they work with silence before starting, and fair to say during the work that a session felt empty. Saying so plainly is part of the work, and it usually opens something rather than closing it down.

Structured, time-limited therapy does something different and does it well. It is built to reduce a specific difficulty within a set number of sessions, and for many people that is exactly right. This way of working is built for patterns that only show themselves when nothing is being organised. There is more about it on my Gestalt therapy page, and on the page about the first consultation.

A quiet session rarely looks like much from the outside. Fifty minutes, a good part of them unhurried, nothing solved and nobody in tears. What people report afterwards is that a sentence from the middle of the silence comes back to them on the way home, in their own voice, and stays for most of the week.

Questions

Common questions

Will the therapist just sit there in silence?

No. Silence is part of the work and it is not used as a test. I say what I notice, ask what is being experienced, and check whether the pace suits. When a pause feels unbearable, that discomfort is itself worth describing, and it usually says something about how silence has worked in a person's life.

How would I know a quiet session was useful?

Usually it becomes clear afterwards. A conversation at home goes differently, an old reaction is caught sooner, or something from the session keeps returning during the week. It is also fair to tell a therapist that an hour felt empty. Saying so is part of the work and tends to be useful in itself.

Is this approach right for everyone?

No single approach suits everyone. Where a set programme with clear exercises is what someone wants, a structured therapy may serve them better. This way of working tends to suit people who are curious about their patterns, have already tried to think their way out of them, and are prepared to go slowly.
