My Partner Won’t Come to Therapy

What working alone can do for a relationship, how to raise therapy with a reluctant partner, and when to bring them in.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

“I’ve asked him three times. He says therapy is for people with real problems.” Some version of that sentence is common in couples work. Sometimes the refusal is flat. Sometimes it is a change of subject, or an agreement that never becomes a date in the calendar.

The practical question is what to do next, and there are more options than most people assume. Attending on one’s own is a legitimate way to work on a relationship, and often a productive one.

What individual work can do for a relationship

Working alone, a person can examine their own side of the pattern without having to defend it in front of the person it concerns. What do I do when I feel unheard: press harder, go quiet, become reasonable and cold? Where was that learned? What happens physically when a partner’s voice rises, or when they go silent at dinner? Those questions are easier to consider when nobody else present has a stake in the answer.

The work also asks something harder: that therapy stops being used to change the other person. It is tempting to arrive with a list of what a partner is doing wrong, and the list has its place. The work tends to go further once attention comes back to the person in the chair. Arnold Beisser’s paradoxical theory of change holds that change occurs when a person stops striving to become what they think they should be and becomes more fully what they are (Beisser, 1970). In a relationship this often means feeling one’s own frustration, hurt or longing clearly, instead of converting it into a campaign.

What people find is usually mixed. Some discover they have carried more than their share for years. Some see how their own withdrawal or criticism helped keep things stuck. Often both. Neither is a verdict on anybody. Each tends to change what actually happens on an ordinary Tuesday evening, which is where a relationship lives.

How one person changes the field

Gestalt therapy understands a person as part of a field. We exist in our relationships and surroundings, and the self is continually shaped by what is around it. Gordon Wheeler argued that the individual is always embedded in relationship and cannot be understood apart from it (Wheeler, 2000).

For a couple, that means a stuck pattern belongs to the space between two people. Neither of them owns it alone. The practical consequence is worth stating plainly: if a pattern is made by two people, a change in one of them changes the pattern. When one partner stops playing a familiar part, the other’s part no longer fits the way it did. It may not be welcomed at first. Something usually moves anyway.

In my book on couples therapy, one composite story follows a woman who attends mostly alone while her husband misses session after session. As she begins to hear her own voice and to set limits at home, the family reorganises around her (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). The story is drawn from many hours of practice rather than from one person. The principle behind it is one I see often.

One caution. This is not leverage. Where somebody changes their side in order to make a partner do something, the old pattern is still running, now with better manners. The changes that move a relationship tend to be the ones made for a person’s own reasons, and kept whatever the other one does.

Raising it so it does not land as a verdict

Where there is still some hope that a partner might come, the wording matters more than people expect. “You need therapy” is heard, reasonably, as a diagnosis delivered by somebody who has appointed themselves the healthy one. “I’ve been going, and I’d like you to come with me once” asks for something small and specific. “I’d like us to understand what keeps happening between us” describes a wish rather than a fault.

Timing matters as much as wording. An invitation issued in the middle of an argument is part of the argument, and will be answered as one. The same sentence on a quiet evening, with no follow-up pressure, is a different sentence.

It helps to name the fears a partner may have without waiting for them to be admitted: of being ganged up on, of being cast as the problem, of what might come out. Many people who refuse therapy are refusing an imagined room in which they are the defendant. Couples work takes both people seriously, and the search for blame gives way fairly quickly to curiosity about the pattern. You might send them the couples counselling page and leave it at that, so they can read how the work proceeds and decide without being watched.

Then leave it there. Asking once and waiting usually works better than asking monthly, and it spares a person the role of the one who keeps pushing, which is frequently the very role that has become exhausting.

When a partner joins later

Partners who said no sometimes come round in their own time, often after noticing that something at home has shifted. How they join deserves some thought. Coming into a therapy that has been running for months can feel like walking into somebody else’s house, and the person who arrives second can feel outnumbered by the history already there. That is a real difficulty, and one that can be worked with when it is named early.

Some couples prefer a different therapist for joint sessions, and a referral is easy to arrange. Others are comfortable continuing with the same one, provided both partners can be taken equally seriously, and that is discussed openly before any joint work begins. Either way, what a person has learned about themselves travels with them when the arrangement changes, and it tends to let couples work move with more clarity.

If a partner never comes, there is still the part of this that one person can act on. A clearer sense of what you want, what you are willing to keep doing and what you can live with is worth having in its own right. It is also the side of the pattern that can be moved directly, which is more than most people in this position believe they have.

Questions

Common questions

Can I work on my relationship without my partner?

Yes, in a meaningful sense. Relationship patterns involve two people, so when one of them changes how they respond, the pattern shifts. Individual therapy is a place to examine one side of the dynamic, what a person feels and needs, and how they might respond differently. It requires nobody else’s agreement, and it often changes the relationship anyway.

How do I ask my partner to come to therapy?

Speak about your own wish rather than their faults. Something like “I’d like us to understand what keeps happening between us” is easier to hear than “you need help”. Acknowledge the fear of being blamed. You might share a description of how the work proceeds, so your partner can read it and decide in their own time.

Can a partner join later if individual therapy has already started?

Often, yes. The first question is whether the same therapist can take both partners equally seriously given the work already done. Some couples are comfortable with that; others prefer a fresh therapist for joint sessions, and a referral can be arranged. What has been learned in individual work usually helps couples sessions move with more clarity.
