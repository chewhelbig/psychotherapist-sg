How Long Does Deeper Therapy Take, and How Would I Know It's Working?

What the early weeks involve, why frequency changes over time, and the quieter signs that the work is taking hold.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 6 min read

At the end of a first session people usually ask how many of these they will need. It is a fair question and it deserves a practical answer.

Most of the answer depends on what is brought. Some people come with something specific: a recent loss, a decision about a relationship, a stretch at work that has shaken their confidence. When a problem has a shape, the work tends to follow it, and it may take a handful of sessions or a few months. Others come because something keeps repeating. The same argument with every partner. A sense of never being enough, however much is achieved. Anxiety that returns whenever life gets close. Patterns of that kind usually took years to form, often in childhood, and they were once sensible ways of coping. They tend to loosen slowly. Short-term counselling, including what many workplaces offer through an employee assistance programme, is built for the first kind of problem and does it well; there is more on that distinction on my after EAP page.

The first few weeks

A first session is mostly orientation. The person says what brought them, I ask about the history around it, and both of us get some sense of whether the approach fits. The questions tend to be oblique: what happens in the chest when a particular subject comes up, what was going on a moment before the voice dropped. That is the register the work runs in, and it is worth finding out early whether it suits. The first consultation page describes that session in more detail.

Over the following weeks, less time goes on background and more on what is happening in the conversation itself. Many people find they have said something in these sessions that they have not said anywhere else. Some feel lighter within a month. Others feel stirred up, sleep badly for a while, or find old feelings surfacing at inconvenient moments. That can be unsettling. It is often a sign that something long kept down is being felt rather than managed, and it belongs in the session rather than being weathered alone.

Weekly at first, monthly later

New clients usually come weekly. Regular contact gives the work continuity and allows what happens between sessions to be followed while it is still fresh. A working relationship that can carry honest conversation takes weeks to build, and weekly meetings build it faster.

Once the work has taken root, many people move to a slower rhythm. Most of my longer-term clients now come once a month. By then they are doing a good deal of the work in daily life, catching their own patterns as they happen and bringing what they caught to the session. A monthly rhythm suits that stage. It also makes long-term therapy sustainable across years rather than months, and it leaves room for life to happen between sessions. Some people return to weekly during a hard stretch and ease off again afterwards. At SGD 220 for fifty minutes, the difference between weekly and monthly is not a small matter, and it belongs in the planning rather than outside it.

How the work is reviewed

Frequency is decided together and returned to. Every so often, and certainly whenever something seems off, I ask what has changed since the work started, if anything, and what is still being carried. The same question can be put to me. A client who thinks we are circling is entitled to say so, and that sentence tends to open something rather than close the work down. A therapy in which it cannot be said is already narrower than it should be.

Decades of psychotherapy research suggest that the quality of the relationship between client and therapist is among the most consistent factors in whether therapy helps (Wampold & Imel, 2015). So one early marker is whether a person can say what is really on their mind in the session, including when something the therapist has said does not sit right. That matters more than leaving each session feeling better. Some of the useful sessions are uncomfortable.

Questionnaires have their place, and anyone who wants to track how they feel week by week can do so. In deeper work the numbers may move slowly, or rise for a time before they fall, because feelings kept out of view are being felt again.

The quieter signs

Change in this kind of work tends to show up sideways. The first sign is often timing. A familiar reaction arrives and it is noticed a moment sooner than it used to be: the flush of anger, the urge to go blank, the pull to smooth things over. The old thing still gets done, and now it is seen being done. That gap is small, and much of what follows starts there.

Later the gap widens into a choice. A difficult conversation is stayed with for one more minute. The awkward thing gets said in the meeting. A silence is left standing instead of filled.

Another sign is how people treat themselves afterwards. The voice that attacks every mistake is often still there; it gets less of the floor, and recovery takes hours instead of days. Relationships tend to shift before anyone can explain why. A partner remarks that they seem less tense. A conversation with a parent goes differently. A friendship that had been quietly tolerated becomes harder to tolerate.

Change often announces itself as a moment that used to go one way and now goes another.

This follows a principle that runs through my work. Arnold Beisser (1970) argued that change happens when a person stops straining to become who they think they should be and becomes more fully aware of who they are. In my book on couples therapy I describe how the harder partners try to force themselves or each other to change, the more entrenched their struggles become (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). Awareness comes first. The shifts follow it, often in ways that are hard to capture on a questionnaire and easy to feel on a Tuesday evening.

What is actually being decided

Ending is part of the work and it is discussed openly. With a focused problem the end is usually clear enough: the thing that brought the person in no longer weighs on them in the same way. With longer work the signs are subtler. Sessions feel less urgent. Reflections outnumber crises. The old pattern can be regarded with some humour. Some people stop there; others keep an occasional session as a place to think. The question of stopping can be raised at any point.

So the thing to weigh is less a number of sessions than two costs set side by side. On one side, a common shape: weekly for two or three months, then monthly for a year or two, which is a real commitment of money and attention and will sometimes be inconvenient. On the other, the pattern that brought the person in, which has probably been paid for in some currency for years without being counted. That comparison is worth making before starting rather than halfway through.

Questions

Common questions

How many sessions will I need?

It depends on what is brought. A focused issue, such as a recent loss or a specific decision, may need a handful of sessions or a few months. Long-standing patterns in relationships or self-worth usually take longer. Frequency and duration are reviewed together as the work goes on, and the question of stopping can be raised at any point.

Do I have to come every week?

New clients usually start weekly, because regular contact helps the work take hold. Once it has, many people move to a slower rhythm, and most of my longer-term clients come once a month. Frequency is decided together and can be adjusted, for example returning to weekly sessions during a difficult period.

How would I know it is working?

Often by timing rather than by mood. A familiar reaction is noticed a moment sooner than before, and later there is a choice where there used to be a reflex. Self-attack takes up less room. Relationships shift before anyone can explain why. Feeling better after each session is a less reliable guide.
