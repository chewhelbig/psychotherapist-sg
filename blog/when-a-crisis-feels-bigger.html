When a Crisis Feels Bigger Than What Happened

When a present loss lands on an older one, the reaction can outsize the event. Steadiness first, then the question of what was touched.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 6 min read

A relationship ends. A partner's messages come to light. A redundancy letter arrives, or a parent dies after an illness everybody saw coming. These are hard events, and most people expect to grieve, to be angry, to be unsteady for a while. What frightens them is the size of it.

They cannot sleep for weeks. They feel about six years old. A breakup after eight months floors them more thoroughly than a divorce once did. They say they do not understand why this is hitting them so hard, and there is often shame in it too, as though the intensity were evidence that something is wrong with them.

Why the reaction can outsize the event

A crisis rarely lands on blank ground. It lands on a person who already carries a history of loss, rejection, danger or being left alone with too much. When the present event resembles something in that history, even faintly, the older experience can wake alongside it. What is felt then belongs partly to now and partly to then, and from the inside there is no seam between them.

In my book on couples work I describe a partner's ordinary withdrawal setting off a terror that turned out to belong to a six-year-old whose father had slowly disappeared (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). Bessel van der Kolk (2014) has written about how the body holds what the mind has set aside, in breath, posture and startle. A betrayal now may be the first thing in twenty years to reach that place. Where the crisis is an affair, my page on infidelity counselling goes into it in more detail.

This is one reason advice to keep things in perspective bounces off. The event is plainly survivable, and can be seen to be. The nervous system is answering a different question.

People known as the reliable one are often the most shaken when something finally gets through. The coping was holding something in place, and when it gives way what it held comes forward, usually at the moment functioning is most required.

What steadies people in the first weeks

None of that is where the work starts. In the early weeks the task is steadiness, and what helps is more concrete than people expect.

Sleep comes first, because everything else is worse without it. The aim during a crisis is enough sleep rather than good sleep. Many people find a fixed waking time does more than a fixed bedtime. Lying awake tends to go better if it is interrupted—twenty minutes up, in low light, with something dull to hand—than if it is spent in bed negotiating. After weeks of broken sleep it is worth raising with a doctor; short-term help is a reasonable thing to ask for.

Then eating, which goes first and is noticed last. Appetite disappears in a crisis, so eating tends to need a schedule rather than hunger to run on. Small and regular, with some protein in it, is enough. A body cannot hold a mood steady on coffee.

Then people. Most of us either tell nobody or tell everybody, and both cost something. What works better for many is choosing two or three and being specific with them, since people want to help and guess badly. Come and sit here on Thursday. Call me at nine. I don't want to talk about it, I want to watch something. Being specific spares the second job of managing everyone's concern.

Then the shape of the day. A crisis dissolves structure, and unstructured time is where the mind goes round. Three fixed points is usually enough: a time to get up, something that involves going outside, and one meal with another person in it. Reversible decisions are generally better postponed. Some cannot wait, a lease or a lawyer or a flight or whether to tell the family, and those tend to go better thought through with somebody than alone at three in the morning.

Three in the morning

Almost everyone in an acute crisis has a version of that hour. The mind offers the whole situation at once, with every worst reading attached. The thinking at that hour feels unusually lucid and is unusually unreliable. Nothing gets solved at three, and the attempt tends to lock the alertness in for another hour.

What people find helps is having decided in daylight what they will do. Paper by the bed, so a thought has somewhere to go until morning. Then getting up: low light, a dull page, something warm to drink. Attention on the body, feet on a cold floor or hands under a tap, brings a nervous system down more reliably than reasoning does. Then back to bed, allowing for the possibility that it happens again at five.

If the three o'clock thoughts turn towards harming yourself, that is the point to contact your doctor or a crisis line the same night. Crisis lines and short-term counselling are built for the acute phase and they do it well. Many people need nothing beyond that, and for some the next step is a few sessions through an employer scheme, which I have written about on the page for people who have finished short-term counselling.

Later, what was touched

Once there is enough ground to stand on, the other strand becomes possible, and it is slower. It might begin with a phrase that keeps recurring, something like I am always the one who gets left, and with staying near it long enough for its history to come into view. It might begin with a sensation, a particular clenching known since school. Nothing is dug up. The older material announces itself through what is happening in the present, and it comes forward a little at a time.

I facilitated a workgroup of trauma researchers reflecting on their interviews with survivors, and wrote about what surfaced there: a great deal of what mattered was felt in the listener's body before anyone could name it (Tóth & Chew-Helbig, 2025). It is one reason a therapist's own responses are worth taking seriously as information.

Over time the old experience can be felt as old. The breakup stays painful, the redundancy stays unfair, the death stays a loss. They stop carrying the full weight of everything that came before them. I would not tell anyone their suffering is a gift; that is too tidy, and usually said by people who are not in pain. A redundancy can damage a family's security and a person's standing, particularly where a visa depends on the job. What can be said is that a reaction larger than the event is pointing somewhere, and that with enough ground underfoot it can be followed.

The sequence matters more than the speed. Sleep, food, two or three named people, three fixed points in the day, and a plan for three in the morning. Then, once those hold, the question of what this has woken. The first sessions after a crisis usually look quite ordinary: what happened this week, what was eaten, who was called, what has to be decided by Friday. The rest waits until it can be borne.

Questions

Common questions

Is it normal to react so strongly to a breakup or a job loss?

Strong reactions are common, and they are not a sign that something is wrong with a person. When a present event resembles earlier experiences of loss or rejection, older feelings can wake alongside the new ones. That can make a reaction feel much larger than the event. Therapy can help tell the two apart.

What tends to help in the first few weeks?

Concrete things. A fixed waking time, eating on a schedule rather than on appetite, two or three people told specifically what is needed, and three fixed points in the day. Reversible decisions postponed. A plan decided in daylight for the three o'clock hour, since nothing gets solved at three.

How is this different from crisis counselling?

Crisis counselling is designed for the acute phase and does that well. The work described here continues past that point, into what the crisis has stirred from earlier in a life. It is slower, it proceeds in phases, and it rests on a relationship built over time.
