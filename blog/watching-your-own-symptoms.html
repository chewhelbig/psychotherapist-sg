Looking at Your Symptoms from the Outside

Stepping out of rumination begins with describing it: when it starts, what it does in the body, what it promises and what it costs.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

I have been in it all week. The account that follows is usually detailed and fluent: the message that was not replied to, the sentence a colleague used in a meeting, the thing a mother said in 2019. It is also entirely from the inside, which is where the difficulty lives.

Rumination is a predicament with a particular structure. Everything inside it is content. Each pass through the material feels close to resolution, which is what keeps the passes coming, and an evening goes. Nobody does this for entertainment. From inside the loop the only available question concerns the subject matter, and the subject matter is inexhaustible.

Inside the loop there is only content

Seen from a step away, a rumination has features its content conceals. It starts at a particular hour, or in a particular chair, or after a particular kind of contact with a particular person. It has a bodily signature—tightness under the sternum, a change in breathing, restlessness in the legs, heat in the face—which many people can recognise once they have language for it. It makes a promise, usually some version of think this through once more and it will finally be settled. And it charges something: the evening, the sleep, the conversation that was only half attended to.

Being able to describe those features is a substantial change of position, and it asks nothing in the way of stopping. That matters, because being told to stop ruminating is useless advice, and most people have already issued it to themselves for years. Beisser's observation was that change follows from becoming fully what one already is rather than from straining towards what one would prefer to be (Beisser, 1970). An accurate description of the pattern is one form of that.

Describing and explaining are different jobs

Explanation asks why. Description asks what, where, when and how much. Explanation is satisfying, and it can become another place to hide: a second loop, one storey up, in which the reasons for ruminating become the new material. Description is duller and it does more.

This is the phenomenological attitude Gestalt therapy inherited. Attend to what appears, set the theory about it aside for the moment, and stay with the appearance long enough for it to show something (Perls, Hefferline & Goodman, 1951). In practice it is very ordinary work, conducted in plain language, and it is the part of psychotherapy that looks least like therapy.

A person's own words

Standard questionnaires have their uses. They give a clinician a comparable number, and they can show change across months. What they are less able to give is a person's own language, and for this work that language is the instrument.

Left to choose their own words rather than a diagnostic term, people arrive at the fog, the tribunal, the spin, the audit, the committee, the tight little room. A word of that kind comes with its own detail, and it tends to be far more accurate than anxiety. I have argued elsewhere that lived experience is carried by aesthetic description, by texture and atmosphere, in a way diagnostic categories do not reach (Chew-Helbig, 2022a). Something similar applies to a person's record of themselves. Moderate anxiety, six out of ten, tells them little the following morning. The fog came in after I read his message, I lost two hours, and I was cold—that tells them where to look.

A measure built out of that language has two advantages. It gets used, and it points at the real object. It might be a mark in a notebook each time the fog arrives, or three words at the end of the day, or a rating of something only that person would think to rate: how far away the room felt, how much of a meal was tasted, how long it took to put the phone down. A generic worksheet asks about a phenomenon somebody else described.

What the pattern is promising

Once a pattern can be described, the question of what it is for becomes askable. Most rumination is doing something. It may be an attempt to prevent a bad outcome by rehearsing it. It may be a way of staying loyal to someone by continuing to suffer over them. It may stand in for a conversation that feels impossible. It may keep grief in a form that seems more manageable than grief.

That question is very hard to ask from inside the loop, and it is one of the things a second person is for. In therapy the pattern can be examined while it is live, since rumination does not confine itself to the evenings and often appears during a session. Attention goes somewhere. The face changes. Looking at it as it happens is a different exercise from reconstructing it a week later, and it is the reason this work is done in the present tense.

Why looking at it helps at all

People reasonably ask why observation should change anything, given that they can see perfectly well the rumination is unhelpful. The difference is one of position. Seeing that a pattern is unhelpful is a judgement made from inside it, and it usually adds a layer of self-reproach. Describing what the pattern does is done from a step outside, and a step outside is somewhere to stand.

It also returns a choice that is invisible from within. Once the fog is a thing that arrives around nine in the evening after a certain kind of message, there is a gap between the message and the fog, and things can go into that gap: a walk, a phone call, the washing up, telling somebody that it has started.

This sits alongside other help rather than in place of it. Severe obsessive thinking often deserves structured treatment as well, and there is more about that on my page on anxiety counselling. The approach behind the observing itself is described on my Gestalt therapy page.

Much of the work is this. Two people looking at the same pattern in the present, in language the person recognises as their own, often enough that it becomes familiar rather than frightening. Getting the description accurate usually takes a few weeks, and it is the part most people underestimate.

Questions

Common questions

Isn't watching my symptoms just more overthinking?

It can tip that way, which is why the description stays concrete: what happened, where it sat in the body, how long it lasted. The question of why is left for later. Overthinking asks for reasons. Description asks for observations. Many people find the difference becomes clear within a couple of weeks.

Why not just use a standard anxiety questionnaire?

Questionnaires are useful for comparing people and tracking change over months. For this work the point is a person's own language, because the words they choose carry detail a scale cannot hold. Both are often used together: a simple rating for the record, and a measure in the person's own terms for the noticing.

Does this work for severe obsessive thinking?

It can help, and it is rarely the whole answer on its own. Where obsessive thinking is severe, structured treatment and sometimes psychiatric input matter, and I will say so and refer. Observational work suits what often remains alongside that: the shame, the isolation, and the pattern's grip on ordinary hours.
