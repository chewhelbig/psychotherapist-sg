Why Does Self-Compassion Make Me Feel Worse?

When kindness stings, it is reporting something. What tends to help is smaller and more particular than the exercise.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Self-compassion is widely recommended, and for many people it works. For others it misfires. They follow the instruction, a hand on the chest and a kind sentence addressed to themselves, and feel worse. Sadder, or more agitated, or faintly embarrassed. Some describe a wave of grief that seems to arrive from nowhere. Others report a hard voice rising up: this is ridiculous, you don't deserve this.

Then comes the second difficulty. The usual response is to try harder. More repetitions, another meditation, another book. When that fails too, the failure joins the pile, and the person becomes someone who cannot even be kind to themselves properly. A practice meant to quieten the critic has handed it fresh material.

Why warmth can raise the alarm

For many people the self has been organised around shame. Being hard on oneself was protective. It kept a person ahead of other people's criticism, useful, unlikely to be caught out. The critic is cruel and it is also a strategy, and it has been running for years without a day off.

A warm gesture disturbs that arrangement. Where softness was unsafe early on, or where care arrived with conditions attached, care arriving now can read as the opening of something whose ending is already known. Something in the person checks for the catch.

Warmth also brings up its own absence. Kindness directed at a part of oneself that went without it makes contact with precisely how long it went without. That is often what the sudden grief is about. People describe it as coming from nowhere. It comes from somewhere quite specific.

Paul Gilbert and colleagues studied this directly and found that some people fear compassion, both offering it to themselves and receiving it from others, and that these fears travel with self-criticism, depression and anxiety (Gilbert, McEwan, Matos & Rivis, 2011). The people most likely to benefit are often those for whom it is hardest to take in. The sting is a response with a history, and it says very little about whether the exercise is being done correctly.

What tends to help instead

When a gesture raises alarm, the more useful question is what settles this particular person, and the answer is more specific than most books allow for. What calms one agitates another. Stillness and closed eyes regulate some people; for someone whose alertness has long been a survival tool they can do the opposite, leaving less to look at and more to imagine.

So the search is for what already works, and there is usually something. For some it is movement—a walk, stairs, swimming—anything that gives agitation somewhere to go. For others it is weight and temperature: a heavy blanket, cold water, a long shower. For others the route is through the hands, in cooking or repair or an instrument. For others it is an animal nearby, which is part of why Mulan is often in my room. Company without conversation settles some people more reliably than any exercise, and so does a task with a clear end.

These amount to a person's own regulating repertoire more than to a set of techniques, and most people have one already, assembled by accident and never noticed. Finding it and using it deliberately tends to be more dependable than importing a practice designed for an average nervous system.

Sensation before verdict

There is also a question of order. Self-compassion practices work mostly at the level of content, a better sentence to replace a worse one. Arguing with the critic on its own ground is slow going; it has been rehearsing those arguments for decades.

Underneath the sentence there is nearly always a physical event that came first. Heat in the face. A drop in the stomach. A jaw setting, a bracing across the back, the particular tiredness that arrives when something has been touched. By the time "I'm pathetic" is audible, the body has already had its say.

Many people find the physical event easier to notice than the verdict is to dispute, and noticing it changes the situation. "There is heat in my face and my throat is tight" is a description. "I'm pathetic" is a sentence one is standing inside. Describing creates a small amount of room, and that room is where anything else becomes possible. Change tends to follow awareness of what is actually happening rather than effort to feel otherwise (Beisser, 1970).

Warmth is first learned between people

Tronick's Still Face studies showed how quickly an infant answers when a caregiver's face goes blank: the reaching, the protest, then the collapse into stillness (Tronick et al., 1978). We come to know ourselves as acceptable, or otherwise, through faces that respond to us. Being kind to oneself usually rests on having been received kindly by somebody.

Where that experience was thin, supplying it alone resembles speaking a language one was never taught. The words are on the worksheet and the body does not recognise them. There is something odd, too, about being at once the person who suffers and the person who comforts. Part of what works when another person is kind to us is that it comes from outside and we did not arrange it. For someone who has always had to look after themselves, self-soothing can feel like one more task done alone.

In psychotherapy, warmth arrives in small amounts and at a pace the person sets, and that is the part which makes it usable. Dose matters more than depth. Over months many people find it becomes less alarming to be treated well, and turning that towards themselves stops being a performance and begins to resemble something they have actually experienced (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). There is more about this on the shame therapy page.

For anyone wanting somewhere to begin alone, the phrases can be set down for a few weeks in favour of attention to what already settles them: the times the agitation comes down a notch, and what was happening just before. Who was there. Where it was. Whether there was movement or stillness. A fortnight of that produces something no book can, which is information about one nervous system rather than the average one.

Questions

Common questions

Is it common to feel worse when practising self-compassion?

It is common enough. For people with a strong inner critic or deep shame, kindness can bring up grief, agitation or embarrassment. Research on fears of compassion describes this pattern. It usually indicates that the harshness has been serving a protective purpose, and that warmth is reaching something that went without for a long time.

What tends to help instead of the exercises?

What already settles a person, which is particular to them: movement, weight, cold water, the hands occupied, an animal nearby, company without talk. Sensation is easier to notice than a verdict is to argue with. Warmth from another person, arriving in small amounts, does the rest slowly.

How does therapy help with a harsh inner critic?

Rather than arguing with the critic, the work gets curious about it: when it speaks, what it protects against, how it registers physically. It also attends to what happens when care does reach someone, and keeps the dose bearable. The critic tends to soften as it becomes less necessary.
