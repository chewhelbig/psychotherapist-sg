Being Understood Is Not the Same as Being Met

Accurate understanding brings clarity. Being met goes further: met by another person, we meet ourselves.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 6 min read

There is a particular loneliness that can follow a good conversation. The explaining went well. The other person, or the app, reflected it back accurately. The sense of being understood arrived. And something stayed exactly where it was.

Versions of this are common. Friends who listen well, journalling that clarifies, worksheets that make sense, late-night exchanges with a machine that is endlessly patient and never tired. All of it helps, some of it a great deal. And still there is a gap, and the gap has a shape.

Being understood

Understanding is accurate reception. What a person says goes in, is organised, and comes back in a form that makes sense. A good friend does this. A well-designed programme does this. A chatbot does it remarkably well, at any hour.

There is nothing wrong with it, and most of us need far more of it than we get. When a friend says "of course you felt that way", something in the chest loosens. When a worksheet shows that the panic follows a sequence, the panic becomes less mysterious. Clarity, perspective, sometimes relief. Experience goes in and comes back tidier than it was.

What is missing is harder to name. The listener tends to stay where they were. Very little changes for them. The most exposing sentence a person has ever said can be answered in the same even tone as a remark about the weather, and some part of the speaker registers that and files it away.

Being met

Martin Buber distinguished between relating to someone as an object to be known and meeting them as a whole person (Buber, 1923/1970). In the first, one stands at a distance and gathers information. In the second, both people are in it, and both come away slightly altered. Gestalt therapy built much of its practice on that distinction, treating the therapist as a participant in the encounter, with a body, a history and responses of their own (Hycner & Jacobs, 1995).

In a recent paper on shame and artificial intelligence I describe a client who used a chatbot between sessions (Chew-Helbig, in press). It reassured him kindly and accurately. What it could not do was remain in the place where he believed everything about him was wrong. Nothing about him mattered to it. It could be neither unsettled nor affected, and it had no way of misreading him and then finding its way back.

AI comforts. Therapy metabolises.

Comfort settles the surface. Metabolising is slower. It is the process by which something that could not be borne alone is taken in, felt in company, and gradually becomes part of a life that can be lived.

Meeting oneself

The part of this that is easiest to miss is also the most consequential. Being met by another person is at the same time an encounter with oneself. If I meet you, you also meet you. Something that has been carried for years without being looked at comes into view, and it comes into view because somebody else is present while it does.

This is the point at which psychotherapy does its deepest work, and it has been studied closely. The Boston Change Process Study Group, examining what actually produces change in therapy rather than what theory predicts, located it in moments of meeting: brief passages in which two people arrive at the same place at the same time, something reorganises, and neither of them planned it (Boston Change Process Study Group, 2010). Explanation is not what does the work. The meeting does.

What gets met in those moments goes by several names. Some people speak of the inner child. Clinicians speak of trauma. I call it the wound we carry, often without knowing we carry it, and much of a life can be arranged around not going near it. It does not resolve by being described accurately. It becomes bearable by being present in the room with another person who stays, and by the person themselves finally being in contact with it rather than managing it from a distance.

That contact is what people tend to describe afterwards as the moment something changed. Not the insight, which often arrived years earlier and changed little. The moment of recognising, in company, what had been there all along.

What becomes sayable

The practical difference shows up in what a person finds they can say. Understanding asks that experience be put into presentable form first. Being met asks for no such preparation, and that is what alters the range of what can be spoken.

Sessions that are steady and private enough allow people to try things out. An idea can be followed to its end without anyone being harmed by it. Some of what emerges is audacious: a thought about leaving, a wish that a parent had died earlier, a suspicion that the life they built was assembled for somebody else's approval. Many people find that a thought of that kind loses a good deal of its charge once it has been said aloud a single time, in a room where nothing happens as a result.

Much of it is far more ordinary. "I am angry." Three words, and a number of people cannot say them without being flooded. They can describe the situation that would make anger reasonable. They can report that anyone else would be angry in their place. The first person, present tense, is what will not come. Where anger was dangerous early on, or where it was answered with withdrawal, the words tend to arrive carrying everything that followed them last time.

Most of us have something of this kind: something that feels dangerous to say aloud, or dangerous to let go of. It need not be dramatic to be stuck. What the sessions offer is that it can be said once, in a considered setting, and what is actually true about saying it now can be found out rather than assumed.

Why it takes another person

Many of the difficulties people bring were formed in relationship, often early, in moments when somebody who mattered looked away or went blank. What was learned then was learned in company, and in my work it tends to revise in company. Reassurance arriving from outside a relationship rarely reaches that learning. A new experience with a present, responsive person reaches it more often.

Such experience accumulates rather than arriving whole. One good moment is a beginning. What seems to do the work is repetition: the discovery that the other person is still there the following week, and the week after, and has not tired of what was said. Shame, as I understand it, is an event between people, and it reorganises between people too.

None of this makes other forms of support less valuable. A friend who understands is a good thing to have, and a tool that helps someone think at two in the morning has its place. They do different work. There is an overview of Gestalt therapy on this site for anyone who wants the approach described more fully.

The distinction is small in the telling and large in practice. Understanding gives a person an accurate account of themselves. Being met gives them access to the part the account was built to leave out.

Questions

Common questions

Can an AI chatbot replace a therapist?

A chatbot can offer information, reassurance and help with organising thoughts, and many people find that useful. What I doubt it can do is be affected by a person, misread them and then repair it. In my work those experiences are where much of the change happens, particularly where shame is involved.

What does being met actually mean in practice?

It means the sessions are steady and private enough that a person can say what they have not said elsewhere, including to themselves. It also means something more: met by another person, we meet ourselves, and research on what changes in psychotherapy locates much of the healing in exactly those moments.

I have supportive friends. Why would I need therapy?

Friends matter, and good friendships carry a great deal. A therapeutic relationship differs in that its whole focus is one person, and it can take what friendships often cannot: the parts kept from people one loves, the patterns that repeat with them, and the slow attention needed to see those patterns clearly.
