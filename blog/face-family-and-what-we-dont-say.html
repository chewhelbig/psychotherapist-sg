Face, Family and the Things We Don't Say

In families where face holds everything together, some things are never said. Psychotherapy is one place they can be.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 6 min read

There is a particular silence that many of us grew up inside. It sits at the dinner table when an uncle's drinking is mentioned and then dropped. It follows a cousin's divorce, a sibling's failed examinations, a parent's long absence that everyone agreed to call work. Nobody instructed us to keep it. We learned it the way we learned which relatives to greet first at Chinese New Year.

My family is not the kind that talks about these things. It is among the more common sentences spoken in consulting rooms here, and a great deal is folded into it. Loyalty. Protection. A lifetime of reading the room. Sometimes a loneliness that has never been given a name.

Face is a real thing

In Singapore, and across much of Asia, face is part of how social life holds together. Scholars of Chinese social relations describe two strands of it: mianzi, the standing and reputation a person or family holds in the eyes of others, and lian, the moral face, the sense of being a decent person who can still look the community in the eye (Hwang, 1987). Face shapes who speaks first, what is said in front of whom, how favours are asked for and returned. It holds relationships in order across generations.

So when a family keeps something quiet, it is often doing what families here have long done to protect one another. A mother who never speaks of her own mother's temper may be sparing her children. A father who will not discuss a business failure may be holding the family's standing in the only way available to him. It is best understood as a form of care, even where it causes harm.

And it does cause harm. What is kept unsaid does not disappear. Many people notice it as a tightness in the chest before family gatherings, or a jaw that sets when certain subjects come near. Over time it shapes whom we marry, how we parent, and what we can bear to hear from a partner. The unspoken becomes a kind of atmosphere that everyone breathes and nobody names.

Shame holds us together, and it can isolate us

Shame has a poor reputation in therapy circles. It is often treated as a disorder of self-esteem, something to be reasoned away. In my book The Shame Paradox I take a different view (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). I understand shame as part of the architecture of our moral life. It tells us that we matter to others and that others matter to us. It is how a child learns where the edges are, and how an adult knows they have hurt someone they love. A person who can feel no shame has lost something human.

Face cultures understand this well. Shame here is also a social mechanism: it keeps obligations visible and behaviour within bounds. The difficulty comes when the mechanism turns inward and becomes the only voice. It can then stop connecting people to one another and begin sealing them off. We become fluent in what should be shown and quiet about what is happening inside.

Gordon Wheeler (2000) argued that Western psychology has tended to picture the self as a solitary unit, when in fact we are made in relationship and live within fields of belonging. That view sits closer to Asian ways of being than much of the counselling literature does. It also means the pain of the unsaid is shared. Where one person in a family carries a secret, the family tends to arrange itself around it.

Why saying it at home is so hard

To speak openly at home can feel like a betrayal. It can bring shame on parents who did their best with what they had. It can shift a person's standing in a family where standing was hard-won. Many of the people I see have tried. They raised something once, felt the room go cold, and understood that the door was closed. Others never tried, because they already knew.

There is often a second layer. People who have lived abroad or married across cultures may find themselves between two sets of rules. A partner from elsewhere asks why one cannot simply tell one's parents, and the question lands as a small humiliation, as though the loyalty were a weakness. Holding both worlds is tiring, and it can be lonely in a way that is difficult to explain to either side.

What cannot be said at home can still be said somewhere.

A room outside the family

Psychotherapy sits outside the family's web of obligation, so what is spoken there costs no one face. There is no audience of relatives, no reputation to manage, and no one present who will be hurt by hearing it. Sessions are confidential. For some people that fact alone changes how they breathe within a few weeks.

The work goes further than disclosure. It addresses what the silence has cost: what could not be felt because it could not be said, and the shape a life takes when it is organised around an absence. It proceeds in phases, at a pace the person sets. Nothing has to be spoken before it is ready to be spoken, and the hesitation itself usually carries its own account of the family it came from.

I practise in Singapore and trained in Vienna, where much of my early work took place across language and cultural divides. I have written about a therapy conducted with a client whose language and history were far from my own, and about how the meeting depended on remaining curious about difference rather than assuming understanding (Chew-Helbig, 2023). Shared culture between therapist and client is a comfort, and it can equally conceal assumptions, which is why I prefer to ask.

What tends to change is quieter than a family confrontation. People find they can hold their loyalty and their own experience at the same time. They stop needing their parents to understand everything in order to feel understood somewhere. Some eventually say something at home, in their own way and their own time. Others never do, and still find the silence weighs less once one other person knows what is inside it. There is more about how I work with this on my page on shame therapy.

The family does not have to change for this to be worth doing. That is the part people find hardest to credit at the start. Some silences grow lighter simply because one other person now knows what is in them. At the next reunion the uncle's drinking still goes unmentioned, and the evening is easier to sit through.

Questions

Common questions

Will therapy make me turn against my family?

No. A family's ways are not treated here as the problem to be corrected. Many people come to hold their loyalty and their own experience side by side. What is done with what emerges, including whether anything is ever raised at home, remains the person's own decision, made at their own pace.

I find it hard to talk about feelings at all. Is that a problem?

It is a very common starting point, particularly for people raised where feelings were not discussed. Sessions need not begin with feelings. They can begin with what is noticeable, or simply with what is difficult to say. The hesitation itself usually tells us something useful, and there is no need to hurry it.

Is what I say in therapy kept confidential?

Yes. What is said stays in the room, within the usual professional limits around serious risk of harm, which I explain at the outset. No family member, employer or insurer hears what is discussed. For many people that is what first makes it possible to speak about something they have never said aloud.
