Shame Rarely Announces Itself

Shame seldom arrives labelled. It is woven into relationship, which is why unease can precede any judgement at all.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 6 min read

Shame is seldom the reason anyone books an appointment. People come about sleep, or temper, or a marriage that has gone flat, or the tiredness of always being the capable one. The word arrives later, if at all, and usually after the thing itself has been recognised in some other disguise.

Shame happens between people

I understand shame as an interpersonal event. It arises in the presence of another person—a face, a tone, a pause before a reply—or of someone imagined, which the body treats much the same way. It needs a witness, even a remembered one.

More than that, I consider shame to be woven into the fabric of relationship. It is part of how being-with-others works, which is why the feeling so often precedes any actual judgement. We are uneasy before the interview, the recital, the difficult email, the walk past a group of strangers. Nothing has happened yet. What registers is the prospect of being seen and assessed.

This is also why shame appears where it makes no rational sense. A waiter's flat expression, a neighbour's raised eyebrow, a stranger's laughter at the next table. These are people whose opinions we would never seek and could not name a week later, and still something tightens. If shame were only about the people who matter, that would not happen. It happens because exposure itself is what the system responds to.

Guilt and shame get used interchangeably, and the difference is worth having clearly. Guilt concerns an action: I did something bad. The self stays intact and there is somewhere to go, an apology, amends, a different choice next time. Shame moves the verdict onto the person: I am bad. Now there is nothing to put right, because the fault is felt to sit in who one is. That shift, from the deed to the self, is what makes shame hard to address directly. It offers no action.

The bridge, and what breaks it

Gershen Kaufman described an interpersonal bridge: the line of trust and mutual interest running between two people, which lets a child reach out and expect something back (Kaufman, 1989). When the reach meets contempt, irritation, distraction or a blank face, the bridge breaks. Shame is what that break feels like from the inside.

A child cannot usually conclude that the parent was exhausted, or frightened, or out of their depth. The child needs the parent to be reliable, and so concludes something about themselves instead. The conclusion is made early and without words, and it tends to hold, largely because nothing came along to contradict it.

Repair matters here as much as rupture. A parent who shouts as a toddler reaches for a hot pan and then picks the child up has done something ordinary and important: the startle, the flush, the tears, and then the return. Shame in that sequence teaches a boundary and leaves the bond standing. Where the return rarely came, shame was learned without its second half. Robert Lee and Gordon Wheeler brought this thinking into Gestalt therapy, treating shame as a signal about the state of our connections, with silence among its main voices (Lee & Wheeler, 1996).

What it wears

The purpose of shame in the moment is concealment. It lowers the eyes, quietens the voice, draws the shoulders in, and it wants the exchange over. An emotion built to hide does not announce itself, so in ordinary life it tends to arrive wearing something else.

Often it wears irritability. A partner makes a neutral remark about the dishes and a flash of heat arrives, far out of proportion to the dishes. It can wear perfectionism: the report rewritten until midnight, because anything less might show something that must stay out of sight. It can wear over-apologising—sorry for asking a question, sorry for arriving early, sorry for standing in a doorway—each apology asking quietly whether the person is still acceptable.

It can wear contempt, which is the most efficient of them. Someone else's error becomes unbearable and the voice goes sharper than was intended. For a moment the defective person is somebody else.

Sometimes it wears nothing at all. A simple question is asked, in a meeting or across a consulting room, and the mind empties. Many people describe the words as having gone. That blankness is usually very old. It is what a body settled on in circumstances where disappearing on the spot was the safest option available.

What these share is a sequence faster than thought. Something is seen, or feels about to be seen. Then comes the move to become less visible, by whichever route a person's history made available.

In a marriage, and in a family

Between partners this becomes visible quickly. One makes a small complaint. The other hears a verdict on who they are and returns a counter-attack, or a cold silence. The first, exposed in turn, escalates. Within a few minutes the argument has left the dishes entirely and neither person can say what it is about. Each is defending against a feeling the other cannot see. Unrecognised, shame tends to get passed back and forth like this, sometimes for years. I have written about it as the pathos of the field: the suffering that fills the space between two people when the bond is broken or in question (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). It sits in the air of a room as much as in either person. There is more on my page about couples counselling.

In Singapore many people grow up with a fine sense of face, of how conduct reflects on parents and family. There is real care in that sensitivity. It can also mean shame is carried on behalf of other people, and kept further out of sight.

What happens when it is named

Because it hides, shame slips past direct questioning. Asking someone whether they feel ashamed usually produces a considered no. It is more available while it is occurring, and what is available then is small: a glance up to check another person's face, a voice dropping halfway through a sentence, a joke, a change of subject, a sudden tiredness. We may notice several of these in a single conversation without connecting them. Much of the work is recognising them as one thing rather than as a collection of unrelated habits (Chew-Helbig, 2026b).

Most people expect that naming it will make matters worse. What tends to happen is quieter than that. The sentence gets said, the thing is out, and very little occurs. The room stays as it was. Nobody leaves. For someone whose old conclusion was formed in a moment when the other person did leave, those unremarkable few seconds are the part worth noticing. Questions

Common questions

How do I know if what I feel is shame?

Shame often appears as an impulse to hide, shrink or disappear. It can show up as blushing, a dropped gaze, blankness, sudden anger or a need to be perfect. A useful sign is the sense that something is wrong with who one is, beyond anything one has done. Most people do not arrive with the word.

What is the difference between shame and guilt?

Guilt concerns an action: I did something wrong, and amends are possible. Shame concerns the self: I am wrong, or not enough. Guilt tends to move people towards repair. Shame tends to move them towards concealment, which is part of why it is so hard to notice and to speak about.

Is it possible to get rid of shame completely?

I do not think that is a helpful aim. Shame is part of how we are built for relationship and belonging, and it has a role in moral life. What therapy can change is the fear of shame, and the way it runs a life from the background, so that it becomes something recognisable and speakable.
