The Successful Person Who Secretly Feels Like a Fraud

Knowing one is competent and being unable to feel it: what sits in that gap, and what psychotherapy attends to.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

A person sets out their own record, because they have been asked for it. The degrees, the promotions, the clients who ask for them by name. They reach the end and shrug. None of it counts. Each item arrives with an explanation attached. The timing was lucky. The panel was kind. The competition was weak that year.

Pauline Clance and Suzanne Imes named this in the late 1970s, having noticed it among high-achieving women who were convinced they had fooled everyone around them (Clance & Imes, 1978). It has since been described across genders and professions. In Singapore it turns up in lawyers, doctors, bankers, academics and founders, and often in people who moved here for a demanding post and now feel watched.

The gap evidence does not close

What makes this condition stubborn is how little it has to do with proof. Favourable feedback gets filed under they do not really know me. A mistake registers at once, as confirmation. A person may know they are competent and be quite unable to feel it. That gap is where the suffering sits, and it tends to widen, because each success raises the cost of being found out.

It is also why advice aimed at thinking runs out so quickly. Most people who bring this to therapy have tried it already, and some can describe the impostor phenomenon more fluently than the clinician can. The fear is older and more bodily than argument. Many people notice it first as a tightening before a presentation, a flush when an unanswerable question arrives, or the rehearsal of tomorrow's meeting at two in the morning. Something similar shows up in work anxiety more generally.

The reflex of qualification

The fraud feeling arrives faster than people expect, in a second or two, and what follows it is quick and well practised. The achievement gets qualified. There was nobody else available. It is less senior than it sounds. Some make a joke. Some change the subject. Some move straight to what could still go wrong. Within half a minute the thing has been taken apart and made safe, and the person has generally not noticed doing it.

The qualifier is performing a function. It heads off an imagined response: a listener who finds them arrogant, or looks unconvinced, or quietly revises an opinion downwards. Gershen Kaufman described the ways people defend against shame, and striving for perfection is among the most socially rewarded of them (Kaufman, 1989). Nobody tells a high performer to slow down, so the strategy goes unchallenged for decades.

Some of us are guilty of this in small daily ways, and it costs little. It becomes a condition worth attending to when the qualifying is constant, when it runs at two in the morning, and when a working life has been quietly arranged to prevent an exposure that never quite arrives.

What stopping would expose

Ask what would be found if the proving stopped, and the answers are rarely about competence. Ordinary. Needy. Too much. Dull. The fear underneath tends to concern being unwanted rather than being incapable, which is much of why further evidence of capability does so little for it.

This is the point at which the difficulty stops being a matter of confidence at work. What is at stake is whether a person can be received at all without having earned the reception first. Many people find they have never tested this, because they have never stopped earning long enough to find out.

I understand shame as part of the architecture of our relational lives rather than a fault to be removed (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). That changes what the work is for. The aim is less the removal of a feeling than a clear account of what the feeling guards, and what becomes possible once that is known. There is more about this on my page on working with shame.

What psychotherapy addresses

Short, structured help can steady this considerably. Learning to describe the pattern, to sleep, and to sit an appraisal without dread is real work and for many people it is sufficient. What tends to bring someone to longer work is the return of the same state at each new level of seniority, in a fresh setting, with the old certainty intact.

Psychotherapy attends to the pattern rather than the episode: how worth came to be conditional, what the striving has protected, and what has never been permitted to be ordinary. It proceeds in phases, from what is near the surface downwards, at a pace the person sets. Sessions are private and steady enough that things can be said in them that are unsayable at work and often unsayable at home.

There is something to observe without any of this. The next time someone says the work was good, notice the sentence that follows. Most of us have one, worn smooth with use, and it comes out at the same speed every time. That half-second before the qualifier is where the whole matter actually takes place.

It is slow, and it should be. A strategy that has held someone together for twenty years does not dissolve in six sessions, and it ought not to be dismantled before something else can take the weight. What shifts first is usually the qualification rather than the feeling. The qualifier arrives, the person notices it arriving, and one day leaves it out, and the room does not change temperature.

Questions

Common questions

Is impostor syndrome a mental health condition?

It is an experience described by Clance and Imes rather than a formal diagnosis: a persistent sense of fraudulence despite clear evidence of competence. It often sits alongside anxiety, perfectionism and exhaustion. In my work I usually find shame underneath it, a fear of being unwanted rather than of being incapable.

Why doesn't praise or success make the feeling go away?

Because the feeling was rarely built on evidence in the first place. Where worth has long felt conditional on performance, each success postpones the fear rather than settling it, and praise can register as a misunderstanding awaiting correction. What tends to help is the experience of being received without having earned it.

I function well at work. Is therapy really for me?

Many of the people I see are highly capable. Functioning well and being at ease are different things. Where a good deal of a person's energy goes into holding themselves together, or into dread of exposure, that is usually worth examining, whatever the working life looks like from outside.
