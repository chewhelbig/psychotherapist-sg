Can a Chatbot Do What a Therapist Does?

A fair account of what AI does well, and the narrower claim a clinician can still make for what happens between two people.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

The question comes up in sessions now, sometimes half as a joke. If the application is free and awake at three in the morning, what exactly is the therapist for? It is a fair question, and it deserves an argument rather than a defence.

The case for these tools is stronger than my profession generally concedes, so it is worth putting first.

What these tools do well

A chatbot is available when nobody else is. It does not tire of the same question asked for the fifth time. It is patient in a way that human beings, therapists included, are not reliably patient. It costs little or nothing. There is no waiting list, no referral, no receptionist to explain oneself to. For many people it is easier to type the worst sentence than to say it to a face, at least the first time, and that lowered threshold does real good.

The content is often sound as well. These systems can put a name to a feeling, describe a breathing exercise that works, set out a cognitive technique accurately, and bring some order to a spiral of thought at the hour when order is hardest to come by. Reassurance lowers panic, and lower panic lets thinking resume. That is not a small thing, and I would not want it taken from anyone.

I would go further. Some of what has been sold as therapy is largely psychoeducation and reassurance delivered at a fixed hourly rate. A machine can probably do that part well, and cheaply, and at three in the morning. If AI takes over that work, I am not sure my field has much to complain about.

The claim I would still make

My claim is narrower than the one usually made on my side of this argument, and I want to state it carefully, because I have heard it stated much too broadly. It concerns one region of the work rather than the whole of it.

In my work, the moments that change something are usually moments of being seen by someone who had the option of turning away. That is a different matter from being told one is acceptable. A person can be told that a hundred times, accurately and warmly, and find the sentence slides off. The difficulty was seldom a shortage of information.

What appears to make the difference is discovering in practice that one can remain in a relationship while believing oneself unacceptable within it, and find the other person still there afterwards. I do not think that can be delivered from outside. My sense is that it has to be lived, in the company of someone who is present, who could leave, and who does not.

An observation from practice

In a paper for the European Journal of Psychotherapy and Counselling I describe a client who brought a third presence into the work (Chew-Helbig, in press). Whenever something felt uncertain in the therapy, he consulted a chatbot, and afterwards he brought the exchanges along. The answers were kind and, as far as they went, accurate. They steadied him, often enough to bring him back to the next session, and I was glad he had them.

They did not settle the question, and I do not think they could have, because the question was not a request for information. Another opinion on whether he was a burden was not what was missing. What appeared to be needed was to be certain of the worst, and then to look up and find the therapy still standing. That happened several times over the course of the work, and I think it did more than any of the reassurance, mine included.

What a person adds

A system is not implicated in the exchange. Anger costs it nothing. It does not misread a person and then have to find its way back to them, which is the sequence by which trust between people ordinarily accumulates. In that paper I put the limit in one line: it cannot be wounded. I hold that as a claim about what these systems are rather than a prediction about what they will always be.

A clinician is implicated. Therapists misread people, tire, and bring their own history and assumptions to the work, and that is a real limitation rather than a hidden virtue. It is also, I think, part of what makes the encounter worth something: an arrangement that could have gone otherwise and did not. A machine can promise safety in principle. Between two people it is established gradually, which is slower and, for the difficulties I am describing, appears to be the point.

Decades of outcome research have pointed at the therapeutic relationship as a major ingredient rather than a pleasant extra (Wampold & Imel, 2015). I read that as support for the narrow version of my claim. It does not show that a person is required for every kind of help, and I would not stretch it that far.

What I am not claiming

I am not claiming that everyone needs long relational work. Plenty of difficulties respond well to short, structured help, and the people delivering it are doing something real. Community services, employee assistance programmes and brief counselling are built to steady distress and to get a person through a period, and they often do exactly that. What tends to bring someone on to slower work is a pattern that keeps returning after the steadying, which I have written about on the page about continuing after short-term counselling.

I am not claiming that using a chatbot is avoidance. Most of the clients I see who use one are using it sensibly. I would rather hear what they asked it and what it answered, because the questions people put to a machine tend to mark the places where they feel most exposed.

And I am not confident about the future. These systems are improving quickly, and arguments of the form no machine will ever have aged poorly before. What I can report is what is observable now, from one small practice.

So the matter ends in a question rather than a verdict. When an application says a person is acceptable, does anything alter? For some it does, and that is worth knowing. Many people find the sentence slides off, and then the useful question is whom they would need to hear it from, and what saying it would cost that person. A system with nothing at stake cannot answer the second half.

Questions

Common questions

Is it a problem if I use ChatGPT or another AI tool between therapy sessions?

Not in itself. Many people use AI to calm down, organise their thoughts or get through a bad night. It can be useful to bring what was asked and what was answered into the sessions. The questions people put to a chatbot often mark exactly where they feel most exposed, which makes them useful material.

Why doesn't reassurance help with shame?

Shame tends to be a bodily certainty about who one is in someone else's eyes, usually formed long before words. Reassurance addresses the thinking mind, so it often slides off. What appears to help is the experience of being seen in the very place a person feels unacceptable, by someone who stays. That takes time.

Can AI tell me whether I need therapy?

It can help describe what is happening and point towards resources, and for some people that is sufficient. What it does not do is register what happens in a person as they speak about something painful. A clinical assessment attends to that, and it is a different kind of information about what might be needed.
