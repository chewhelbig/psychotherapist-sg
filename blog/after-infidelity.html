After Infidelity: When the Relationship You Knew No Longer Exists

An affair ends the relationship both partners thought they had. What that crisis holds, and what therapy can offer.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

The day everything changes

Discovery often comes by accident. A message on a shared tablet, a receipt, a friend’s awkward silence. Within a moment the relationship a person thought they were in no longer exists. The past rearranges itself. Anniversaries, business trips and ordinary evenings all have to be looked at again.

The weeks that follow are frequently described as a storm. Sleep is usually the first thing to go. Many people find themselves checking phones and retracing calendars at three in the morning. The mind circles the same questions: why, since when, who knew, what did that person have. There can be a rage that frightens its owner, and a numbness that frightens them more. There is often a strong pull to keep the family steady for the children, alongside a sense that falling apart is a luxury nobody can afford.

A crisis in the whole relationship

Infidelity is a relational crisis. It touches nearly everything at once: trust, sexuality, family, friendships, money, confidence in one’s own judgement. For the partner who was betrayed, among the most corrosive experiences is humiliation. In my book on couples therapy I describe humiliation as shame imposed by another person’s actions, the experience of being diminished and exposed by what someone else has done (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). Where friends knew first, the humiliation compounds.

Comparison with the third person can become consuming: their age, their body, their work, whatever they apparently offered. Sexuality, which may already have been tender ground, becomes charged with images and questions. And many of the assumptions that held a life together give way at once. I knew this person. I would have noticed. Losing beliefs of that kind can feel like losing one’s footing in the world.

In Singapore the circle of people affected is often wide. Parents and in-laws may know, or may have to be kept from knowing. Friends may have taken sides, or been asked to keep secrets. The question of face, of what can be said and to whom, adds isolation to an already lonely time.

The partner who had the affair

The one who had the affair is often cast, by others and by themselves, as the person with nothing to feel. Many carry considerable shame, alongside fear of losing their family and confusion about their own choices. Some rush to put it right with promises, apologies and a wish to move on quickly. That urgency can be a flight from despair, and it tends to land badly with a partner who is still in shock.

Esther Perel has written that affairs are frequently less about the partner than about a longing within the one who strays, for aliveness, or for a lost version of themselves (Perel, 2017). This explains without excusing. Responsibility for the choice stays with the person who made it. Understanding what the affair meant can still matter to both people, whatever they decide.

Therapy without a fixed goal

Many couples arrive with one urgent question: can this marriage be saved. It is a fair question, and it need not be the first one. What is usually needed in the early weeks is room for what has happened to be felt and spoken, in the presence of someone who can take it without taking over. The question why may have to be asked many times, and there is no advantage in hurrying it. Some answers help. Some questions have no answer that will satisfy, and part of the work is finding what a person needs when an answer falls short.

Couples therapy after an affair does not advocate for a particular outcome. Both partners are taken seriously, which is a different matter from treating what each of them did as equivalent. There are stretches in which the betrayed partner’s needs plainly come first and are named as such. There are others in which the partner who had the affair needs room to face what they have done without collapsing into self-justification or self-hatred. The direction belongs to the couple, and the pace belongs to whichever of them can bear least at the time.

Shame runs through all of it. In The Shame Paradox I argue that shame is part of what makes us moral and relational beings, a signal that something between people has been broken (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). Both partners may be flooded with it. Left unspoken, it drives attack and hiding. Said aloud in conditions steady enough to carry it, it can become part of how two people meet again. The work attends to what is happening physically as much as to the story, because in this crisis the body usually knows first: the held breath, the turned shoulder, the moment someone goes somewhere far away.

What may come after

Some couples rebuild. When they do, the old relationship is rarely restored. The one they knew has ended, and whatever follows is new, made by two people who now know more about each other, including the painful parts. Others decide to separate, and therapy can help them do that with dignity, which matters particularly where children mean the parenting relationship will continue for decades.

Some people come alone first, because a partner is not ready, or because they need somewhere to think before any joint conversation. That is a reasonable place to start. Sessions are private, and nothing about the affair has to be recounted before a person is able to speak of it. You can read more on the infidelity counselling page, and about couples counselling more generally.

Most of the work in the early months is unglamorous. The same three questions come round again, someone stops breathing at the same point each time, and the conditions stay steady enough that something true can eventually be said. The larger decisions tend to arrive later, and they arrive more easily once the worst of it has been spoken aloud at least once.

Questions

Common questions

Can a marriage survive infidelity?

Some do, and some do not. When a couple rebuilds after an affair, it is usually a different relationship from the one before, made with more knowledge of each other. Therapy need not begin with that decision. The first task is usually to let what has happened be spoken and felt, so that any later choice rests on firmer ground.

Can therapy help the partner who had the affair?

Yes. That partner often carries shame, fear and confusion alongside responsibility. Therapy is a place to face what was done without collapsing into excuses or self-hatred, and to understand what the affair meant. That understanding matters whether or not the relationship continues, and it usually matters to the other partner as well.

Should a couple come together or separately after an affair?

Either can be right. Some couples come together from the start. Sometimes one partner needs individual sessions first, to steady themselves or to think before a joint conversation. What matters is that the work is paced so neither person is overwhelmed. The options can be talked through at a first consultation and decided from there.
