Why Can't I See My Own Blind Spots?

A blind spot is not visible from where one stands. Some patterns become apparent only in the presence of another person.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Most people who consider psychotherapy are observant about themselves. They have noticed patterns, read widely, reflected in journals and in long conversations with friends. And something goes on happening that the reflection does not reach: friendships that fade in the same way each time, managers who all turn critical by the second year, a partner who says you are never really here to someone who feels entirely present.

A blind spot is, by definition, not visible from where one stands. Looking inward more carefully seldom brings it into view, because the looking is done from inside the same position. Some patterns become apparent only in the presence of another person, which is an awkward fact about self-knowledge and a useful one.

Why the inside view has limits

This is no failure of intelligence or effort. The patterns that shape our relationships were mostly laid down before we had words for them, in how we were held, looked at and answered. They do not feel like patterns. They feel like the way things are, which is much of why they are hard to detect. We see through them, as we see through our own spectacles.

They are also, for the most part, not thoughts. They are actions: what we do with another person while we are talking to them. Moving closer. Pulling back. Pleasing. Testing. Going reasonable. Deflecting with a joke at the moment the conversation turns serious. In research on therapeutic dialogue I have described this second layer as implicit action, and proposed that the way a person interacts within a single conversation reflects their wider patterns of relating (Chew-Helbig, 2019).

Because these moves are performed rather than thought, introspection has little to work with. Nothing turns up in the mind to be examined. The material was in what happened between two people, and by the evening it has gone, leaving only an account of the conversation, composed by the person who made the moves.

How they become apparent

Other people register them. Usually they do not say so. Colleagues, friends and relatives make small adjustments around a person's pattern, speaking more carefully, giving up on a subject, drifting, and the adjustments are rarely reported back. What does get said tends to arrive in an argument, in a form too heated to be used, or in a resignation letter, or at the end of a marriage. By then it is evidence rather than information.

Which is why the same observation can be unusable from one source and useful from another. From a stranger it lands as an attack. From someone with a long acquaintance, no stake in winning the exchange and no plans to leave, it can be considered. Hycner and Jacobs describe this attention to what passes between two people as the heart of a dialogic approach in psychotherapy (Hycner & Jacobs, 1995).

The other route is repetition. A pattern that has crossed three jobs, four friendships and two marriages has announced itself, and the common factor is not difficult to name. Many people arrive at therapy having named it already and being no further forward. Knowing what one does is not the same as catching it in operation, and it is the catching that alters anything.

What blind spots protect

A pattern that survives decades is generally doing something. The distance others notice may once have kept a household manageable. The cheerfulness that tires a partner may have held a family together through a period when nobody else could. The habit of agreeing at the first sign of conflict may have been the only available move in a house where anger was dangerous.

Seen that way, a blind spot is less a defect than an old arrangement still running, and the blindness is part of the arrangement. Noticing the pattern would mean meeting what it was built to prevent. This is why insight on its own so often disappoints. A person may describe precisely what they do, in accurate detail, and go on doing it, because the description has not reached the thing being protected.

What has to become possible is the experience the arrangement was designed to avert: disagreement that does not end an attachment, being disappointed in without being dropped, wanting something openly. Many people find this is the slow part, and it is the part that decides whether anything holds.

What this means practically

Psychotherapy is built for this. It provides a relationship of a particular kind, continuous, private, and outside anyone's family or career, in which patterns of relating show themselves rather than being reported on afterwards. Sessions are steady enough that what is noticed can be said plainly, including the observations that would cost too much to make to a spouse or a colleague. Observations are offered to be tested against a person's own experience rather than handed down as verdicts.

It asks a certain discipline of the clinician, whose own responses are part of what is being observed and can mislead as easily as inform. I have written about the therapist's reflexive account of their experience as a source of clinical knowledge, and about the care its proper use requires (Chew-Helbig, 2022a). A therapist's impression is a piece of evidence about a shared situation, not a ruling on the person in front of them.

Practically, this requires continuity. A single conversation can produce an insight. A pattern needs enough time to appear more than once, in different weather, with the same person present on both occasions. That is slower than a course of skills-based sessions, which serve many people well for other purposes and which I have written about on my after EAP page. It is also why the work proceeds in phases, at a pace the person sets, rather than by putting the difficult material first.

There is one thing that can be attempted alone. In the next difficult conversation, attend less to what is being said and more to what is being done while saying it: moving closer, making the joke, going reasonable, checking the other face for permission. Most of it will be missed. The little that is caught is usually the part that no amount of reflection on a quiet evening would have produced.

Questions

Common questions

Why can't I find my blind spots through self-reflection?

Reflection is done from inside one's own point of view, which is precisely where a blind spot sits. Patterns of relating are actions rather than thoughts: they consist of what we do with another person while talking. There is little for introspection to examine afterwards, which is why they persist despite considerable insight.

Will a therapist simply tell me what my blind spots are?

A verdict handed over is rarely much use, and it is easy to get wrong. It is more useful to point to something that has just happened and examine it together, so that the pattern is recognised from a person's own experience. What is arrived at that way tends to stay, where a diagnosis of character does not.

Why does this kind of therapy take longer?

A pattern has to appear more than once, in different circumstances, with the same person present on each occasion, before it can be seen clearly rather than described. Seeing it is also only half the work: what it has been protecting has to become bearable. That proceeds in phases, at the pace the person sets.
