Good Daughter, Good Employee, and No One Home

Meeting every expectation and still feeling hollow: a Singapore reflection on duty, face and the self that was set aside.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Consider someone a family relies on. She telephones her parents each evening, manages the hospital appointments, remembers every auntie's birthday. At work she is the safe pair of hands, promoted twice, trusted with the difficult clients. Colleagues call her capable. Some call her remarkable.

What is harder to account for is the flatness that arrives when she is alone, in the car park or the shower. Nothing is wrong. Everything is handled. And there is no one at home.

She is a composite. The predicament is common enough in Singapore, in women and men, across communities. The particulars vary. The flatness is recognisable.

Duty is also love

This is easily told as a story about controlling families, and that telling usually misses the point. Filial care, loyalty and the wish to honour one's parents are among the more valuable things a culture can carry. In my couples book I write about belonging that does not have to be earned or proved, and about what many Western families have traded away in pursuit of individual freedom (Chew-Helbig, 2026a).

Face matters too. Kwang-Kuo Hwang described how face and favour organise social life in Chinese societies: standing is held in the regard of others, and relationships are sustained by obligations honoured over years (Hwang, 1987). It is a sophisticated arrangement. It holds people together. It also asks a great deal of them.

Many of the people I see are proud of their families and would do it all again. They love their parents. They know what their parents gave up. The tiredness sits alongside that love without cancelling it.

The difficulty begins when meeting every expectation becomes the main way a person knows how to exist.

The self that got set aside

Some children learn early that their place in the family rests on being good. Good results, good behaviour, no trouble. Their own wants, moods and doubts are quietly put away, because there is no room for them, or because a parent is already stretched thin. The child becomes skilled at reading what others need.

That skill travels well. It makes a reliable employee, a thoughtful friend, a devoted daughter or son. What it tends to leave undeveloped is a felt sense of one's own wanting. Ask such a person where they would like to eat and there is often a small pause, and a glance at the other face at the table.

Gordon Wheeler argued that the self forms within a field of relationships, in contrast to the Western picture of a separate individual who later enters society (Wheeler, 2000). That view sits comfortably here. It also goes some way towards explaining the emptiness. Where a self has been shaped almost entirely around the expectations of others, meeting those expectations may leave surprisingly little over.

High-functioning emptiness

This rarely presents as a crisis. It presents as competence. Many people find they are tired in a way that sleep does not touch. Small personal decisions bring a disproportionate anxiety, while emergencies feel oddly calm, because someone else's need supplies the instruction. Achievements land briefly and drain away. A flicker of resentment appears and is followed at once by guilt.

Underneath there is often shame, quiet and persistent: a sense that the person beneath the performance would fall short. I understand shame as part of how we are built for belonging, a measure of how much the people around us matter (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). Here it has been doing its work too well, keeping a person so attuned to others that they lose track of themselves.

Many who arrive in this condition describe it as anxiety, and that is often accurate as far as it goes. It is also, frequently, the first signal that something has been left out of a life for a long time.

The emptiness tends to surface at a turning point. A parent falls ill. A promotion arrives and means less than it should. A marriage settles into logistics. Children leave home, or a friend asks, kindly, what one does for oneself, and no answer comes. Such moments can be alarming. They are also often the first occasion in years on which a person's own experience has asked for a hearing.

What the work addresses

Psychotherapy of this kind does not require anyone to reject a family or set down their responsibilities. It attends to what was left out: the preferences, irritations and wishes that were put away early and have gone on operating quietly ever since. Awareness comes first, and change tends to follow it rather than being manufactured.

The work proceeds in phases and at a pace the person sets, beginning near the surface and going further as that becomes possible. Sessions are private and unhurried, which is usually enough for a person to say things they have had no occasion to say anywhere else, including the ungrateful ones.

It is common to arrive expecting to have to choose between loyalty and a self. Many people find the choice dissolves. The relationships need not end. They can come to include the person inside them. There is more about this kind of work on my page on shame therapy.

What tends to surprise people is the order in which things return. Irritation comes first, usually at something small and current. Then preferences, of the dinner and weekend sort. The wanting comes later, and it is quieter than expected. People brace for something large and buried. What turns up is a dislike of a particular meeting, and a wish to leave a gathering at nine.

Questions

Common questions

Will therapy make me go against my family?

No. Family, duty and loyalty have a real place in people's lives, and the work does not treat them as the problem. The aim is the recovery of a person's own feelings and wishes, so that their relationships include them as well. Many people find family bonds become warmer once they are no longer carried entirely by obligation.

Why do I feel empty when my life looks successful?

Where a self has been organised around meeting expectations, success can feel like the completion of someone else's list. Wants and feelings may have been set aside so long that achievements do not register as one's own. The emptiness is usually a signal rather than a fault, and it is the kind of thing psychotherapy attends to.

Do you understand Asian family dynamics?

I practise in Singapore, and much of my clinical and research work concerns culture, family and shame, including face, filial duty and intergenerational expectation. I do not assume in advance what a family means to the person in front of me. That is something we establish together, along with what the loyalties hold and what they cost.
