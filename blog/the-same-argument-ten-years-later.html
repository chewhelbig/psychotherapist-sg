The Same Argument, Ten Years Later

The topic changes. The shape of the fight does not. What a recurring argument carries, and what couples therapy addresses.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

The argument both partners know by heart

Couples who have been together for years often describe a single argument that never resolves. The topic varies. Money is common. So are housework, the phone that comes out at dinner, the weekly visit to his parents. What stays the same is the shape of it. One voice rises. The other goes quiet, or cold, or leaves the room. Some hours later there is a truce, and some weeks later the whole thing begins again, almost word for word.

Most couples in this position have already tried to fix it. They have read about communication, learned to speak in “I” statements, agreed to take turns. Those skills are useful, and short-term counselling often teaches them well. Ten years on, the argument is still there, as though it had a life of its own. In a sense it does.

What the argument carries

The subject of a recurring fight is seldom the whole of it. When one partner says, for the fortieth time, that she is the only one who notices the laundry, the laundry is real. Underneath it there is usually something harder to say: that she does not count in this household. When her husband answers that he works long hours and cannot think of everything, the hours are real too. Underneath, for many people, is the old sting of being found lacking, of standing once more in front of a disappointed parent.

Money fights frequently carry questions of power, and of who is allowed to want what. Fights about in-laws carry loyalty, and the particular smallness of being diminished in front of someone else’s family. Fights about phones carry a plain longing to be looked at. These currents seldom get spoken. It is far easier to argue about the dishwasher than to say aloud that one is afraid of not mattering.

A pattern with two authors

A recurring argument works something like a dance. Each partner has steps, and each step answers the other’s. She pursues, he retreats. His retreat makes her pursue harder, and her pursuit sends him further away. Asked who started it, each can point convincingly at the other. Both are right, and neither is.

Gestalt therapy draws on field theory, which holds that behaviour belongs to a situation as much as to a person. In my book on couples work I describe relationship problems as creative adaptations within a wider field—histories, families, culture, money, the size of the flat, the child who wakes at night—rather than as deficits of skill (Chew-Helbig, 2026a). Each partner learned their steps somewhere, usually for good reason. Put together, they make a pattern neither would have chosen.

This has consequences for blame. If the pattern belongs to the space between two people, the question of whose fault it is loses much of its grip, and a more useful question takes its place: how two people, together, keep arriving at the same moment.

Coming close and stepping away

Relationships that work are relationships that move. Partners come close, step away to rest and restore, then return. Rita and Robert Resnick describe this as a cycle of connection, a rhythm between withdrawal, contact, intimacy, temporary merging and protective isolation (Resnick, 2020). Every phase has its use. Difficulty tends to begin when a couple settles into one phase, or when the two are in different phases at the same moment: one reaching out just as the other needs to pull back.

The same argument is often that mismatch, repeated for years. Reaching can be received as pressure. Withdrawal can be received as abandonment. An ordinary need for space, or an ordinary wish for closeness, hardens over a decade into a fixed position. Underneath the stuckness there is commonly some shame: at being too much, or at never being enough. Shame is among the harder things to name, which is part of why an argument stays on safer ground for years.

What couples therapy addresses

Couples therapy is less concerned with adjudicating the latest fight than with the pattern that produces it. Patterns of this kind are reliable. They tend to show themselves in the sessions fairly soon, in the way two people talk about them, and they can then be examined while they are running. My research on therapeutic dialogue looked closely at that level of exchange, the implicit action beneath the words, and at how the way partners speak with each other reflects their wider patterns of relating (Chew-Helbig, 2019).

The work proceeds by slowing those moments down. Much of what keeps an argument running happens faster than speech: irritation arrives before the words do, and one partner has gone flat before either has registered it. Many couples find that seeing what they are doing, and what it protects, makes the next step less automatic. Change of this kind follows awareness rather than instruction, and it tends to arrive in small increments.

Couples sessions are 80 minutes, which allows for that slower pace without either partner feeling hurried past what matters. The sessions are private and unhurried enough that things ordinarily left unsaid at home can be said in them. Nothing has to be disclosed before it can be borne, and the work moves in phases: what recurs, then what it costs, then whatever has been waiting underneath it. You can read more about the arrangement on the couples counselling page.

None of this makes the old topics disappear. Couples who have done good work still disagree about money. What changes is how long it takes them to find each other afterwards, and what a disagreement is allowed to mean about them. In my experience the couples who struggle most are often the ones working hardest to erase their differences, and the ones who find some intimacy are the ones who learn to live alongside them.

A decade of the same argument is usually a decade of the same unspoken sentence, waiting for a steadier moment to be said.

Questions

Common questions

Why do couples keep having the same argument?

Recurring arguments usually carry more than their topic. Beneath money or housework there is often a sense of not mattering, of being controlled, or of being found lacking. Over years each partner’s response to the other hardens into a pattern. Seeing that pattern as something two people make together is more useful than settling whose fault it is.

Is this just a communication problem?

Communication skills help, and many couples benefit from learning them. When the same argument survives those skills for years, something deeper is usually involved: old sensitivities, shame, and a stuck rhythm of reaching and withdrawing. Couples therapy slows those moments down and examines the pattern itself, which tends to reach places that skills alone do not.

How long does couples therapy take?

It varies. Some couples notice a shift within a few sessions, particularly once the pattern becomes visible to both of them. Patterns that have run for a decade usually take longer to loosen. Couples sessions are 80 minutes, which allows an unhurried pace, and how often to meet is reviewed regularly rather than fixed at the start.
