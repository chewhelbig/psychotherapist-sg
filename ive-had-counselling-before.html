I've Had Counselling Before. Why Am I Still Here?

Short-term counselling examines a problem and relieves it. Psychotherapy goes deeper, in phases, at a pace the person sets.

Nicole Chew-Helbig, PhD · Gestalt psychotherapist, Tiong Bahru · 5 min read

Many people who come to a psychotherapy practice have already had help. Six sessions through work. A few months with a counsellor at a community centre. An app, for a while. It helped: sleep improved, a difficult quarter passed, feelings acquired names. And then the same heaviness returns, or the same argument at home, or the same sense of watching one's own life from slightly outside it.

Underneath the enquiry there is often another question. Did I do it wrong? Am I the sort of person therapy does not work for?

In my experience neither is the case. The earlier help usually did what it was built to do.

What counselling and EAP are built for

Short-term counselling, whether through an employee assistance programme, a community service or a low-cost clinic, examines the presenting issue. Something has tipped over. A redundancy, a bereavement, a panic attack in a meeting, a marriage in trouble. Counsellor and client look at that difficulty directly, work out what can be changed, and find relief from it. Sessions are few, often capped, and organised around something nameable in the first meeting.

That relief is real, and for many people it is enough. A good counsellor can help someone sleep again, return to work or see a situation clearly within a handful of sessions. Accessible short-term help does a great deal of good. Nothing here is a criticism of it. The two kinds of work are built for different jobs, and knowing which job is wanted saves a good deal of time.

Depth arrives in phases

Psychotherapy proceeds differently. It works from the surface downwards, in phases, at a pace the person sets. It is not an excavation, and nobody is asked to produce their history at the start. The invitation is plainer than most people expect: bring your life into the session, whatever is in it that week, and see the depth the work goes to.

The material arrives on its own. An argument, a decision that has to be made by Friday, a mood that will not lift, a conversation that went wrong in a familiar way—ordinary events carry the pattern inside them, and it becomes visible as the weeks accumulate. The early phase is unremarkable and is meant to be. We look at what is happening now, in circumstances that can be described without difficulty. Later phases reach further, and they reach further because the ground has been laid rather than because a schedule calls for it.

This is also why longer work carries no fixed number of sessions. Some people come for a few months, others for a year or more. The phases are not stages to be completed; they are depths that become available as trust accumulates.

Why the pace is the method

The word I find most accurate for this is titration. In a laboratory one solution is added to another drop by drop until a change registers, and the slowness is the point, because it keeps the reaction controlled and readable. Suffering can be separated out in much the same way. A small quantity is brought into awareness at a time, felt while there is support for it, and allowed to settle before anything further is added.

Awareness is what does the separating. Many people arrive with their difficulty in one undifferentiated mass, the marriage and the childhood and the job and the shame about all of it, and describe it as a weight rather than as anything with parts. Attention taken slowly enough begins to sort it. This piece belongs to last Tuesday. This piece is much older. This one belongs to my father and has been travelling with me since I was nine.

Gestalt therapy holds a counter-intuitive view of change, which Arnold Beisser set out more than fifty years ago: we change by becoming more fully what we already are, rather than by straining to be something else (Beisser, 1970). Effort to feel differently tends to tighten the knot. Awareness of what is actually happening tends to loosen it, gradually and without force.

Where explanation stops

Many people who come after earlier counselling can explain themselves very well. They know where the anxiety comes from. They can trace the perfectionism to a parent and the accommodating to a household that needed them to be easy. The account is accurate, and it has changed very little.

This is a familiar gap and it is worth stating plainly. Insight describes what is happening. Closure, where it comes, tends to come through experiencing something in the session rather than through explaining it: an old feeling arriving in the present, in small quantity, in the company of someone steady enough to stay while it passes through. That is a different event from understanding, and it is the one that seems to settle things.

Shame is the clearest instance I know. I understand it as part of how we are built for relationship, a signal that belonging matters to us (Chew-Helbig, 2026b). It rarely appears when asked about directly, and it tends to keep clear of structured conversations with an agenda and a goal sheet. It needs time before it can be spoken at all, and often the person does not know it is there.

Research across many kinds of therapy keeps returning to the relationship between client and therapist as one of the elements that most reliably matters (Wampold & Imel, 2015). Longer work takes that seriously. A relationship with time in it is what allows the depths to open in order, and what makes a slow pace tolerable rather than frustrating.

Anyone weighing whether to go further than their earlier counselling may find it useful to read about therapy after EAP sessions. Short-term counselling examines a presenting problem and relieves it, which is often exactly what is needed. Psychotherapy is built for what remains once that relief has been found and the pattern has returned. Working out which of the two is wanted is most of the decision.

Questions

Common questions

Does needing more therapy mean my earlier counselling failed?

Usually it means the earlier counselling did what it was designed for. Short-term work examines a presenting problem and finds relief from it, which is real. Difficulties that have been running for years tend to need a longer frame, where depth is reached in phases rather than addressed in a first session.

How is longer-term psychotherapy different from EAP sessions?

EAP sessions are brief, capped and organised around a named problem. Longer work has no fixed endpoint and proceeds from the surface downwards at a pace the person sets. It resembles a titration more than an excavation: a little is brought into awareness at a time, and nothing is forced before there is ground for it.

How long does this kind of therapy take?

It varies. Some people come for a few months, others for a year or longer. Sessions are weekly at first and reviewed as the work goes on. I do not set a number at the start, because the phases open at their own rate and hurrying them tends to send the material back out of reach.
